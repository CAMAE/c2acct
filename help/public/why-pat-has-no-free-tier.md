PAT has no free tier, and that is a deliberate design decision rather than a pricing posture. Everything PAT produces — comparable scores, meaningful benchmarks, alignment evidence that firms and vendors can actually rely on — depends on data contributed by participants who take the work seriously, and benchmark quality in particular depends on committed contributors. Membership comes in two paid tiers, Pro and Elite; this article explains what that choice protects and what each tier represents.

## Serious data requires serious participants

A shared evidence base is only as good as the people contributing to it. PAT's assessments ask a firm to reflect honestly on how it actually operates — its workflows, its automation, its data flows, its governance, its strategy — and the value of the resulting scores depends on those answers being considered rather than clicked through. The benchmarks PAT publishes are built from member contributions, which means every careless response that entered the pool would dilute the comparison for everyone else.

This is the heart of the membership philosophy: serious data comes from serious participants. A free tier is an open door, and an open door admits casual traffic — people running through an assessment out of curiosity, half-answering to see what happens, abandoning midway. None of that is malicious, but all of it would sit in the same pool as the deliberate, honest work of committed members. PAT chose to keep that door closed. Paid membership acts as a commitment filter: everyone contributing to the evidence base has decided the work is worth doing properly, because they have decided it is worth paying for.

The point is not exclusivity for its own sake. It is that the product PAT sells is, in a real sense, made by its members. When you look at a benchmark or an alignment picture inside PAT, you are looking at the aggregated seriousness of other participants. Protecting that seriousness is protecting the thing you joined for.

## What membership means in PAT

Joining PAT means joining a shared evidence base, not just licensing a piece of software. Each of PAT's three roles contributes something and draws something. Firms assess their own operations and receive a structured view of where they stand. Vendors declare what their products are built to do and receive alignment evidence — how declared capability meets firm-experienced reality. Consultants and ecosystem owners see the alignment picture across the set of firms they manage. Every role's view is built, in part, from other members' contributions.

That mutual dependence is why membership is framed as participation rather than access. A member is not simply a reader of PAT's outputs; a member is one of the reasons those outputs mean anything. The paid tiers exist to keep that circle honest — a community of participants who have each made the same commitment to contribute real signal.

It is worth saying plainly what membership does not buy. It does not buy a better score. PAT's scoring is deterministic arithmetic: the same answers always produce the same result, for every member, at every tier. Nothing about paying more changes how you are measured. What the tiers differentiate is not the measurement but how much interpretive depth surrounds it.

## What Pro represents

Pro is the working membership — the full expression of what PAT is for. A Pro membership represents the decision to participate: to put your firm's operations through a structured assessment, or your product's capabilities into structured declarations, and to stand in the shared evidence base alongside everyone else who has done the same. Pro members contribute to and draw on the alignment picture that is PAT's reason for existing.

Think of Pro as the tier of commitment. It says a firm is prepared to look at itself honestly across five pillars, or that a vendor is prepared to state its capabilities in a form that firm experience can verify. That willingness is the entry price that matters most, and Pro is its formal expression.

## What Elite represents

Elite represents a deeper relationship with the same evidence. It does not change what is measured or how — the assessment, the arithmetic, and the resulting scores are identical at every tier. What Elite adds is deeper interpretive surfaces around those results: for example, seeing where a result sits within a distribution rather than only against a summary comparison, or following a trajectory over time rather than reading a single point.

The distinction is between knowing your score and understanding your position. A score tells you where you are on a 0-to-100 scale within a pillar. Position within a distribution tells you what that number means among the results around it. Trajectory tells you whether the picture is moving, and in which direction. Elite is for members who want that added layer of context — the interpretive depth, not a different truth.

Framed as philosophy rather than feature list: Pro is participation, Elite is perspective. Both tiers stand on the same measurements, contribute to the same evidence base, and are held to the same standards of data quality.

## What the tiers cover, in practical terms

At concept level, Pro covers the working substance of PAT membership: the assessment itself — for firms, the five-module, 100-question structured assessment — together with the scores it produces, the bands that lead their presentation, and the core insights that accompany them. A Pro member has the complete measurement experience. Every question, every pillar score on the 0-to-100 scale, every band from Early through Leading, and the essential reading of what those results say: all of it belongs to Pro. Nothing about the measurement is held back for a higher tier.

Elite's additions are interpretive surfaces layered on that same substance, and three of them give the flavor. The first is position within a distribution of peers: not only a score, but where that score sits among the results around it. This surface appears only when a benchmark actually publishes under PAT's suppression rules — Elite deepens real evidence; it does not manufacture context where the underlying data is too thin to support it. The second is trajectory: how scores are trending across assessments, for firms that re-assess over time and want to read the movement, not just the moment. The third is category-level context around the results. None of these change what was measured or how. They change how much interpretive company a result keeps when it arrives.

## How the benchmark rules back the membership philosophy

The membership philosophy is not merely asserted; it is enforced by publication rules that decide what comparisons members ever see. PAT publishes a benchmark cut only when the cohort behind it has at least five contributors and no single contributor supplies more than 25% of the data. A cut that fails either condition shows as suppressed — PAT declines to render a number rather than display one too thin or too lopsided to carry meaning. Contributors are anonymous within benchmarks, and the data boundaries are strict: production, pilot, and demo data are kept separate, and demo or pilot material never enters a published benchmark.

Read those rules alongside the paid-only decision and a single coherent stance emerges. The suppression thresholds protect readers from comparisons built on too little; the absence of a free tier protects the pool from contributions made too casually. One rule governs quantity and concentration, the other governs commitment, and both serve the same end: when a benchmark does publish inside PAT, a member can trust both that enough serious participants stand behind it and that no single voice dominates it. A membership model that admitted casual data would put constant pressure on those thresholds; the paid tiers relieve that pressure at the door.

## A membership paced without pressure

A paid membership might be expected to come with pressure — a platform anxious to prove itself extracting engagement on a schedule. PAT's cadence philosophy points the other way. Engagement is deliberately paced, with quarterly rhythms as the natural tempo, because firm operations change on the timescale of quarters, not days, and a measurement instrument gains nothing from being refreshed faster than the reality it measures. Reminders, when they come, are deliberately no-guilt: informative nudges about what is ready or worth revisiting, never shame mechanics designed to manufacture activity.

That restraint is consistent with how PAT treats its own outputs. A score is a reading at a point in time, not a permanent label, and assessments can be re-taken as operations change. Membership, in other words, is built for a long relationship at a humane pace — commitment without compulsion.

## Your data remains yours

Membership is paid, but what a member contributes does not become PAT's possession. Tenants' data belongs to them. If a tenant leaves, their data is removed, and the removal is receipted — the departure is documented, not merely assumed. The one thing that persists is what has already been made irreversibly collective: anonymous benchmark aggregates that passed the suppression rules remain aggregate, and individual contributions cannot be reconstructed from them.

For a prospective member weighing the commitment, this is the other half of the bargain. The paid model asks you to take the evidence base seriously; the stewardship model promises that the evidence base takes your ownership seriously in return. Joining means contributing real signal to a shared pool — it does not mean surrendering your firm's data to it.

## The tradeoff, accepted openly

Declining to offer a free tier has a cost, and PAT accepts it knowingly. Some prospective members would prefer to try before committing, and some casual visitors who might eventually have become serious participants will walk away at the sight of a paid-only model. That is real, foregone reach.

The alternative cost was judged worse. A benchmark diluted by uncommitted contributions fails quietly — it still renders a number, but the number carries less than it appears to. PAT already declines to publish comparisons that are too thin to be meaningful; admitting a stream of casual data would push more of the evidence base toward that threshold rather than away from it. Between reach and reliability, PAT chose reliability, on the view that a smaller pool of committed participants produces something worth belonging to, while a large pool of casual ones produces something that only looks like evidence.

There is also a fairness dimension. Members who answer carefully are entitled to know that the comparisons they see were built by people doing the same. A paid-only model is a promise from every member to every other member: what you are reading was contributed in good faith, by someone with something at stake.

## What this means if you are considering joining

If you are weighing PAT membership, the absence of a free tier tells you something useful about what you would be joining. It tells you the benchmarks you would consult were contributed by committed participants. It tells you the alignment evidence a vendor receives, or the assessment picture a firm receives, rests on data from people who took the exercise seriously. And it tells you that PAT would rather be smaller and trustworthy than large and noisy.

The decision to join, then, is less like choosing a software plan and more like deciding whether the work of honest self-assessment — or honest capability declaration — is worth doing at all. Pro is the tier for making that commitment; Elite is the tier for members who want the deepest interpretive view of what comes back.

## Why PAT works this way

The absence of a free tier is best understood as part of PAT's measurement instrument, not as a pricing posture. Everything a member consults inside PAT — benchmarks, alignment evidence, the comparative meaning of a score — is built from other members' contributions, so the numbers everyone compares against are only as good as the care behind the answers. A free tier optimizes for volume over signal: it grows a pool fastest by admitting exactly the contributions least likely to have been made with care. PAT's designers judged that a comparison pool should grow the other way — more slowly, from participants who have each decided the work is worth doing properly — because reach can be added to a trustworthy pool later, while signal diluted at the door is hard to recover downstream.

The benchmark suppression rules apply the same reasoning at the publication end, and they carry a protective purpose the membership door cannot supply on its own. Small-cell suppression — declining to publish any benchmark cut with fewer than five contributors — is the same discipline statistical agencies apply to their own tables, and for the same reason: below a minimum group size, a "group" number is barely distinguishable from someone's private data. The dominance cap, which suppresses any cut where a single contributor supplies more than 25% of the data, stops one participant's results from quietly steering a figure presented as a peer group. Read together, the paid-only door and the suppression gate are one stance enforced at two points: commitment governs what may enter the evidence base, and the thresholds govern what the evidence base may claim to show.

## A closer look: one scenario, start to finish

Consider an invented firm — call it Calder & Finch, a mid-sized practice weighing PAT membership. The first thing the partners notice is that there is nothing to sample: no free tier, no trial account. That absence is itself information. It tells them that everyone whose data they would be comparing against made the same deliberate decision they are now considering, and that the benchmarks inside are not padded with the half-finished assessments of the curious.

Calder & Finch joins at Pro and sits the five-module, 100-question assessment with the care the commitment invites. When the firm later consults a benchmark, one cut publishes — behind it stand at least five contributors, none supplying more than a quarter of the data — while a narrower cut the partners were curious about shows as suppressed. The suppression reads at first as a limitation, then as reassurance: PAT declined to render a number rather than show one too thin to mean anything, which is exactly the discipline the firm's own contribution now benefits from.

A year and several re-assessments later, Calder & Finch moves to Elite, wanting more interpretive company for results it has come to rely on: position within a distribution of peers where benchmarks publish under the suppression rules, and trajectory across its accumulating readings. Nothing about the firm's measurement changes with the upgrade — the same answers would produce the same scores at either tier. What changes is how much context surrounds each result when it arrives. That is the membership model working end to end: commitment at the door, honest publication rules in the middle, and interpretive depth as the optional last layer.

## Common misconceptions

One misreading is that a paid-only model must mean the product cannot survive a free trial. The constraint runs the other way: it is the evidence base, not the software, that cannot survive casual participation. A benchmark pool diluted by uncommitted contributions fails quietly, and PAT keeps the door closed to protect the pool every member draws from.

A second misconception is that Elite members are measured more favorably. They are not, and cannot be: PAT's scoring is deterministic arithmetic, identical at every tier, and the same answers always produce the same score. The tiers differentiate interpretive surfaces around a result — never the result itself.

A third is that paying for membership transfers ownership of a member's data to PAT. Tenants' data belongs to them; if a tenant leaves, the data is removed and the removal is receipted. Only anonymous benchmark aggregates that already passed suppression remain, and individual contributions cannot be reconstructed from them.

A fourth is that Elite always shows a firm's position within a peer distribution. That surface appears only when a benchmark actually publishes under the suppression rules — at least five contributors, no single one above 25%. Elite deepens real evidence; it does not manufacture context where the data is too thin.

A fifth is that a paid commitment implies a demanding engagement schedule. PAT paces engagement deliberately, with quarterly rhythms as the natural tempo, and its reminders are no-guilt by design — informative nudges, never shame mechanics.
