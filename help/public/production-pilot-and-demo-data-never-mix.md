PAT keeps three kinds of data — production, pilot, and demo — strictly separated, and only production data can ever reach a published benchmark. Demonstration and synthetic data exist so people can explore the platform; pilot data exists so participants can trial it; neither is ever allowed to touch the numbers real firms and vendors rely on. This separation is not a policy we enforce by vigilance after the fact — it is a boundary built into how PAT treats data from the moment it arrives.

## Three kinds of data, three different jobs

Performance Alignment Technology (PAT), a Patalign product for the accounting ecosystem, recognizes three classes of data, each with a distinct purpose.

Production data is the real thing: assessments genuinely completed by firms about their own operations, and capability declarations genuinely made by vendors about their products. It is the material from which real scores, real alignment measurements, and published benchmarks are built.

Pilot data comes from trial use — participants working through the platform before their engagement is fully live. Pilot work is valuable to the people doing it, and it looks and behaves like the real thing from the inside, which is exactly why it must be labeled as what it is and kept apart from what it is not.

Demo data is illustrative by design: demonstration and synthetic content that lets a prospective member see how assessments, scores, and comparison surfaces look and feel without anyone's real information being involved. Demo data is honest about being fake — its entire job is to show the shape of the product, not to describe any actual firm.

Each class does necessary work. The rule is simply that each class does only its own work.

## The promise: nothing synthetic ever reaches a real benchmark

The boundary matters most where data becomes shared truth: benchmarks. PAT benchmarks exist to give firms and vendors comparison context drawn from real participants. That context is only worth having if every contribution to it is real. So the rule is absolute: demo and synthetic data never enter published benchmarks, and neither does pilot data. Only production data — genuine assessments from genuine participants — can contribute to a benchmark anyone sees.

Consider what the alternative would mean. A benchmark quietly padded with synthetic records would still look like a benchmark. The chart would render, the comparison would feel informative, and the reader would have no way to know that part of what they were comparing themselves against never existed. That is precisely the failure PAT is designed to make impossible. A comparison built on invented contributors is not a weaker version of insight; it is the absence of insight wearing its costume.

The same logic covers pilot data, even though pilot data comes from real people. Trial-stage answers are produced under trial conditions — participants finding their footing, exploring how questions work, sometimes answering to see what happens. That is legitimate and useful trial behavior, and it is not the same thing as a considered assessment of a firm's actual operations. Letting it into published benchmarks would dilute the very thing benchmarks promise.

## A wall by design, not a cleanup by policy

Plenty of platforms would address this with a policy: a rule in a document saying test data should be excluded, applied by someone remembering to filter it. PAT's position is that a boundary this important cannot depend on remembering. Production, pilot, and demo data are strictly separated as classes — the separation is how the system treats data, not a chore performed on it afterward.

The practical difference shows up in what does not have to happen. Nobody has to sweep synthetic records out of a benchmark before publication, because synthetic records were never in the pool from which benchmarks draw. Nobody has to audit a published cut for stray pilot contributions, because pilot data does not flow toward publication in the first place. When exclusion is structural, the failure mode of a missed cleanup simply does not exist.

This is what we mean when we describe the data boundary as a promise-as-design. The promise is only as good as the mechanism behind it, so the mechanism is the design itself: the classes are walled from one another, and the wall does not have an "unless someone forgets" clause.

## Why this matters if you're evaluating PAT

For a firm considering PAT, the boundary answers a fair question: when I eventually compare my assessment results against a benchmark, what exactly am I being compared to? The answer is: real production data from real participants, nothing else. Not demonstration content, not synthetic filler, not trial-stage answers. The comparison context you would use to understand your own position is made entirely of the same kind of committed, genuine data you would be contributing yourself.

For a vendor, the same boundary protects the meaning of alignment evidence. Evidence that your product's declared capability holds up in firms' experience is only persuasive because the experience side is real. A vendor gains nothing from a favorable number built partly on synthetic inputs — and loses everything if anyone could suspect it was. The wall between data classes is what makes PAT's evidence worth showing to someone skeptical.

For consultants and ecosystem owners, who look at alignment structure across a managed set of firms, the boundary means the picture is composed of actual firms' actual signals. A cross-firm view is a decision-support surface; it can only support decisions if none of its inputs are placeholders.

## Exploring safely: what demo data is for

The strict wall is also what makes exploration comfortable. Because demo and synthetic data can never leak into anything real, a prospective member can look around PAT's surfaces — see how five-pillar results present, how bands frame scores, how comparison views are laid out — without worrying that exploratory clicks are contaminating something. Demo data is a sandbox with a locked gate, and the lock faces both ways: nothing real gets in, nothing fake gets out.

The same comfort applies to pilots. A participant trialing PAT can engage fully, knowing that trial-stage activity stays in its lane. When an engagement becomes fully live, production data starts fresh as production data — real answers, given as real answers, feeding real results.

## One journey through the three classes

Trace an illustrative firm from first contact to full membership and the wall becomes easy to picture. As a prospect, the firm explores PAT through demonstration surfaces built on demo data: it can see how a five-module, 100-question structured assessment presents, how bands frame scores, and how comparison views are laid out — all on synthetic content that describes no real firm. Every click of that exploration stays in the demo class. None of it exists anywhere a real number is made, so the firm can poke at everything without leaving a mark on anything.

The firm then trials the platform. Its pilot-stage answers come from real people at real keyboards, but they are trial answers, produced while finding footing — so they live as pilot data: useful to the firm's own trial, visible in their trial context, and barred from publication. When the engagement becomes fully live, production data begins fresh — considered answers, given as the firm's actual account of its operations, scored by the same deterministic arithmetic as everyone else's.

Only that third trail, the production one, can ever flow toward a published benchmark — and even then only inside a cohort of at least five contributors with no single contributor supplying more than a quarter of the data. The demo exploration and the pilot trial were never candidates. Nothing about the firm's earlier, looser engagement has to be found and cleaned out later, because it was never in the pool to begin with.

## The boundary holds across a data lifetime

Separating data by class answers the question of what kind of data can reach what surface. A second boundary question runs along the axis of time: whose data is it, and what happens to it when an engagement ends? PAT's answer starts from ownership. A tenant's data belongs to that tenant — a firm's assessment material is the firm's, a vendor's declarations are the vendor's — and that ownership does not lapse because the data happens to sit on PAT's side of the screen.

When a tenant leaves, their data is removed, and the removal is receipted: the departing tenant gets confirmation that the removal happened, not a quiet assurance that it probably did. A departure boundary enforced by receipt belongs to the same family as the class wall — a promise backed by a mechanism rather than by trust in someone's tidiness.

One category behaves differently, for a reason worth understanding. Anonymous benchmark aggregates that already passed PAT's suppression rules remain aggregate after a contributor departs — and they can remain precisely because of what they are: individual contributions are not reconstructable from them. What persists is comparison context that never identified anyone; what is removed is everything that was identifiably yours. The two outcomes are not in tension. They are the same boundary logic applied to two different kinds of material — one that carries a participant's information, and one that, by construction, no longer carries anyone's.

## The same discipline guards the content door

Data classes are not the only place PAT refuses to rely on after-the-fact cleanup. The content PAT itself serves — learning and assessment module material — passes through a door with the same character as the data wall. Content must cite authoritative sources to enter the system at all; unsourced content is rejected mechanically at import. Nothing sits inside the system hoping an editor eventually notices it lacks backing, for the same reason no synthetic record sits in a benchmark pool hoping a filter eventually catches it: the wrong material is refused at the entrance, not escorted out later.

Beyond the sourcing gate, content goes live only after a named human review sign-off, and that review discipline is recorded — who reviewed the content, and when. A mechanical gate plus an accountable human decision, both leaving a trace, is the pattern this article has been describing throughout: important boundaries in PAT are structural first. Whether the material is a firm's assessment data or the question content an assessment is built from, the rule at the door is the same — what gets in is controlled at entry, so what comes out can be trusted without an audit.

## One boundary among several

Data-class separation is one of a family of integrity protections in PAT, and it works alongside the others rather than instead of them. Published benchmarks additionally require a cohort of at least five contributors with no single contributor supplying more than a quarter of the data, so small or dominated cuts are suppressed rather than shown. Signal-integrity checks accompany scores so readers know how much weight a result can bear. And evidence lineage means displayed numbers trace to their sources — which is also how the data boundary stays visible rather than taken on faith: a number's lineage runs back to its source data class, so production results demonstrably rest on production inputs.

Each protection answers a different threat. Suppression guards against comparisons too thin to mean anything; integrity checks guard against low-quality response patterns; the data wall guards against the wrong kind of data entering at all. Together they serve one aim: when PAT shows you a number, the number deserves your trust.

## Why PAT works this way

The data wall is one expression of a broader conviction: the numbers everyone compares against are only as good as the care behind the answers. That conviction is why PAT has no free tier — membership is paid, Pro and Elite, by design. A free tier optimizes for volume over signal, filling the pool with casual participation, and benchmark quality depends instead on committed participants whose answers describe operations they actually run. The class wall and the membership model are two halves of the same quality decision: keep uncommitted data out of the pool, and keep the pool made of people with a stake in answering carefully.

Suppression thresholds carry the same logic to the moment of publication. Small-cell suppression is the discipline statistical agencies use, and PAT applies it for the same reason: below a minimum group size, a "group" number is barely distinguishable from someone's private data, and the dominance cap stops any single contributor's results from steering a figure presented as a peer group. So even after the wall has guaranteed that only production data can reach a benchmark, a cut publishes only when at least five contributors stand behind it and none supplies more than a quarter of it. The wall governs what kind of data may enter; the thresholds govern when the entered data is enough to mean something. A benchmark that clears both is real twice over — real in origin, and real in weight.

## How this looks for a firm, a vendor, and a consultant

For a firm, the wall shapes each stage of engagement differently. As a prospect you explore surfaces built on openly synthetic demo content; as a trial participant your pilot answers serve your own trial and nothing beyond it; as a live member your production answers are the only ones that ever feed your real results or, within suppression rules, a benchmark.

For a vendor, the wall means the alignment evidence you receive was measured entirely against production reality. No demonstration record and no trial-stage answer sits behind a delta you might show a skeptical prospect — which is exactly what makes the delta worth showing.

For a consultant or ecosystem owner, the wall means every cross-firm view you work from is composed of actual firms' actual production signals. A comparison surface meant to support decisions contains no placeholders, because placeholders were never in the pool it draws from.

## A closer look: one scenario, start to finish

Give the illustrative firm from the journey above a name — call it Alder & Hale, invented purely for illustration — and follow it one stage further than membership. As a prospect, Alder & Hale explores PAT on demo surfaces: synthetic assessments, synthetic scores, synthetic comparison views, all honest about being fake. During its trial, its pilot answers — real people, trial conditions — stay in the pilot lane, useful to the trial and barred from publication. When the engagement goes fully live, production data begins fresh: the firm works through the five-module, 100-question assessment as its actual account of its operations, and deterministic arithmetic turns those answers into scores and bands.

In time, Alder & Hale's production data becomes eligible to contribute to benchmarks — anonymously, and only inside cohorts of at least five contributors where no single contributor supplies more than a quarter of the data. Years later, suppose the firm leaves. Its data is removed, and the removal is receipted: the firm receives confirmation that it happened. What remains is only what never identified it — anonymous aggregates that already passed suppression, from which individual contributions are not reconstructable. From first curious click to final departure, nothing the firm did in one data class ever leaked into another, and nothing that was identifiably the firm's outlived the relationship.

## Common misconceptions

"Pilot data is treated as worthless." Pilot data is valuable — to the participant whose trial it serves. The wall only means it never flows toward published results, because trial-stage answers are not considered assessments of actual operations.

"Someone at PAT filters test data out before a benchmark publishes." Nobody performs that cleanup, because the design makes it unnecessary. Demo and pilot data are never in the pool from which benchmarks draw, so there is nothing to sweep out and no sweep to forget.

"When a firm goes live, its pilot answers convert into production data." They do not. Production data starts fresh when an engagement becomes fully live. Answers given while exploring how questions work remain trial answers; the firm's real record begins with its real, considered responses.

"If a cohort is too small, PAT tops it up so the benchmark can publish." A cohort below five contributors — or one where a single contributor exceeds a quarter of the data — shows as suppressed, full stop. PAT never pads a cut with anything, synthetic or otherwise.

"When a contributor leaves, benchmarks they touched are retracted." Aggregates that already passed suppression remain aggregate, because individual contributions are not reconstructable from them.

## Questions this article answers

What are PAT's three data classes? Production data (genuine assessments and declarations), pilot data (from trial-stage use), and demo data (demonstration and synthetic content for exploration). Each class does only its own work.

Can demo or synthetic data ever reach a published benchmark? Never. Only production data — genuine assessments from genuine participants — can contribute to a benchmark anyone sees.

Why is pilot data excluded from benchmarks if real people produced it? Because trial conditions produce trial answers — exploring, testing, finding footing — which is legitimate but not a considered assessment of a firm's actual operations.

Is the separation a policy someone enforces, or part of the system? It is structural. The classes are separated in how PAT treats data from arrival, so exclusion does not depend on anyone remembering to filter anything.

What is a firm compared against when it uses a PAT benchmark? Real production data from real participants, in cohorts of at least five contributors with no contributor above a quarter of the data — never demonstration, synthetic, or trial-stage material.

Can a prospect exploring demo surfaces contaminate real data? No. Demo data is sealed in both directions — nothing real enters it, nothing synthetic leaves it.

What happens to a tenant's data when they leave PAT? It is removed, and the removal is receipted — the departing tenant receives confirmation. Only anonymous aggregates that already passed suppression remain, and individuals are not reconstructable from those.
