PAT publishes a benchmark comparison only when the group behind it is both large enough and balanced enough to trust: at least five contributors, with no single contributor supplying more than 25% of the data. A cut that fails either test is not published with a caveat or a warning label — it simply shows as suppressed. This article explains those two rules in plain terms, and why a benchmark you cannot see is sometimes the most honest thing PAT can show you.

## Why a benchmark is only as good as its smallest cut

A benchmark sounds like one big number, but in practice it is many small ones. The moment you compare against "firms like mine," you are slicing the data — and every slice makes the group behind the number smaller.

Small groups create two problems at once. The first is fragility: when only a handful of contributors sit behind a figure, one unusual contributor can drag the whole comparison toward itself, and the "benchmark" stops describing a group and starts describing an accident of who happened to be in it. The second is exposure: contributors to PAT benchmarks are anonymous, and anonymity thins out as groups shrink. In a very small cut, a reader with some outside knowledge could start guessing whose data they are looking at.

Most benchmark problems are really small-cut problems. So rather than publishing shaky numbers and hoping readers apply the right skepticism, PAT draws a hard line: cuts that fall below the line do not publish at all.

## The contributor floor: at least five

The first rule is a minimum head count. A benchmark cut publishes only when at least five contributors stand behind it.

The reasoning is straightforward. Below that floor, a comparison is less a benchmark than a glimpse of a few individual results wearing a benchmark's clothing. With one or two contributors, the "group" figure is barely distinguishable from someone's private data. The floor guarantees that any number you compare yourself against genuinely summarizes a group — and that no contributor's result sits thinly veiled inside a published figure.

The floor applies to the cut, not just the dataset as a whole. A large, healthy pool of contributors can still produce narrow slices with only two or three participants in them, and those slices stay unpublished no matter how big the pool around them is.

## The dominance cap: no single voice above a quarter

Head count alone is not enough, which is why there is a second rule: no single contributor may supply more than 25% of the data behind a published cut.

Imagine a cut that clears the floor with room to spare, but where one contributor accounts for most of the underlying data. Arithmetically there is a group; practically, the "benchmark" is mostly a mirror of that one contributor. Anyone comparing against it would really be comparing against a single organization without knowing it — and that contributor's data would dominate a figure others treat as the group's.

The dominance cap prevents this. By keeping every contributor's share at a quarter or less, PAT ensures a published benchmark reflects a genuine plurality. Both tests must pass together: enough contributors, and no contributor too loud. Failing either one is enough to keep a cut from publishing.

## A worked illustration: three cuts against the same two tests

Consider three illustrative benchmark cuts, each a slice of comparison data that firms might want to measure themselves against.

The first cut has six contributors, and no single one of them supplies more than a quarter of the data behind it. Six clears the contributor floor of five; the balance satisfies the dominance cap. This cut publishes.

The second cut has eight contributors — comfortably above the floor — but one contributor supplies roughly a third of the underlying data. Head count alone would let it through; the dominance cap does not. Because one voice exceeds 25% of the data, the cut shows as suppressed, no matter how healthy its contributor count looks.

The third cut has four contributors, each supplying an even share. The balance is impeccable, but four is below the floor of five, so this cut is suppressed too.

The two tests are independent, and each is binding on its own: failing either one keeps a cut from publishing. And none of the three outcomes is permanent. If the second cut's data rebalances as more contributors join, or the third cut gains a fifth contributor, those cuts publish. The bar never moved — the data cleared it.

## What you see when a cut is suppressed

When a comparison fails the floor or the cap, PAT shows the cut as suppressed. Not an error, not a blank, not an approximate number with an asterisk — a clear indication that this comparison exists as a category but does not currently have enough balanced data behind it to publish.

It is worth sitting with why this is the design. The alternative — publishing the number anyway, with a disclaimer — quietly fails, because numbers outlive their disclaimers. A figure gets quoted in a meeting, lands in a document, and travels on without the fine print that was supposed to qualify it. Suppression means there is no under-supported number to travel in the first place.

So a suppressed cut is not PAT withholding something from you. It is PAT declining to tell you something it cannot stand behind. Encountering suppression is normal, especially in narrow slices, and it carries no judgment about anyone in the cut — it is purely a statement about the size and balance of the data.

Suppression is also a state, not a verdict. The rules describe the data as it stands, not the cut forever: a comparison that shows as suppressed today is one that publishes once enough contributors stand behind it, with no single contributor supplying more than a quarter of its data. The bar never moves for anyone; what changes is whether a given cut has cleared it.

## What these rules protect

The suppression rules protect three things at once.

They protect the contributors. Firms and vendors put real assessment data into PAT, and they do so as anonymous participants in benchmarks. The contributor floor and the dominance cap are what keep that anonymity meaningful in practice — no thin cut where identities become guessable, no dominated cut where one participant's data effectively stands exposed as "the benchmark."

They protect the readers. Every benchmark figure PAT publishes has cleared the same two tests, which means you never have to wonder whether the comparison in front of you is secretly three firms, or secretly one firm in a group costume. If it published, it passed.

And they protect the benchmarks themselves. Comparison context is only useful while people trust it, and trust in published numbers is built cut by cut. This is also why PAT benchmarks are comparison context and never rankings or league tables — the purpose is to help you locate yourself, not to line anyone up for scrutiny.

## Quality of answers, not only quantity of contributors

The suppression rules govern how many contributors stand behind a benchmark and how balanced their shares are. A separate discipline addresses the answers themselves: PAT runs response-pattern quality checks — straight-lining detection is one example — and the resulting signal-integrity information accompanies scores, so a reader knows how much weight a result can bear.

An integrity flag is information, not an accusation. It does not assert that anyone answered carelessly or in bad faith; it marks a response pattern that deserves a lighter grip when reading the numbers built on it.

Together, the two disciplines cover both dimensions a comparison can fail on. Suppression guards the shape of the group — enough contributors, none dominant. Signal integrity speaks to the quality of what those contributors put in. A benchmark reader gets the benefit of both: no under-supported group figure ever publishes, and the scores feeding the system carry their own quality signal with them.

## The same bar at every depth of membership

PAT's membership tiers differ in how deeply results are interpreted, not in whether the suppression rules apply. Elite membership adds interpretive surfaces — for example, where a firm sits within a distribution of peers — but that distribution view appears only when the benchmark behind it publishes under the same rules as everywhere else: at least five contributors, none supplying more than a quarter of the data.

There is, in other words, no tier that buys a look at a suppressed cut. A comparison without enough balanced data behind it is suppressed for everyone, at every level of membership. What a deeper tier deepens is the interpretation of numbers that have already earned the right to appear — never access to numbers that have not.

## Anonymity that outlasts membership

Contributors to PAT benchmarks are anonymous, and that anonymity is designed to hold over time — including after a contributor leaves.

PAT's data stewardship position is that tenants' data belongs to them. When a tenant leaves, their data is removed, and the removal is receipted rather than merely promised. What remains afterward are only the anonymous benchmark aggregates that had already passed the suppression tests — and those stay aggregate: individual contributions are not reconstructable from them.

For anyone weighing whether to contribute assessment data, that closes the loop the suppression rules open. The contributor floor and the dominance cap keep anonymity meaningful while you participate; data removal with a receipt, and aggregates that cannot be unwound into individual contributions, keep it meaningful if you go. Contributing to a benchmark is not a permanent exposure — it is participation in group figures that never point back at you.

## Reading a published benchmark with confidence

Every benchmark figure that appears in PAT has already cleared the same two tests — at least five contributors, none supplying more than a quarter of the data — so a reader can take publication itself as a statement of adequacy. There is no need to interrogate each figure's provenance one by one; that interrogation happened mechanically, before anything reached the screen.

Published figures also sit inside PAT's wider evidence discipline. Displayed numbers trace to their sources — PAT calls this evidence lineage, and its rule of thumb is that there are no orphan numbers. A benchmark comparison is therefore never a free-floating statistic: it is an aggregate with a defined, rule-checked group behind it, presented as context for locating your own results rather than as a ranking of anyone.

Reading one well takes only two habits: treat it as context, not a league table — that is what it was built to be — and remember that the contributors behind it are anonymous, so the figure describes a group, never identifiable peers.

## Integrity as a design choice, not a policy

Notice what kind of protection this is. It does not depend on an editor reviewing figures, on judgment calls, or on anyone deciding case by case what feels safe to publish. The floor and the cap are rules the system applies the same way every time, to every cut, for everyone. That consistency is the point: benchmark integrity is not a promise PAT makes about its behavior, it is a constraint PAT builds into what can appear on a screen at all.

For a prospect weighing whether PAT's comparisons can be trusted, that is the short answer. Every published benchmark stands on at least five anonymous contributors, none of whom supplies more than a quarter of its data — and everything that cannot meet that bar stays visibly, deliberately suppressed until it can.

## Why PAT works this way

The suppression thresholds are not an invention of PAT's — small-cell suppression is the same discipline statistical agencies apply when they publish aggregates. Below a minimum group size, a "group" number is barely distinguishable from someone's private data, so the honest move is not to publish it at all. The contributor floor of five is PAT's version of that minimum. The dominance cap answers the discipline's second concern: a number presented as a peer group must not be steerable by any single contributor, so no one voice may supply more than a quarter of the data behind a published cut. Together they make "published" mean something specific — a genuine group, with no participant dominant inside it.

The absence of a free tier is part of the same story. PAT's membership is paid tiers only — Pro and Elite — because benchmark quality depends on committed participants. A free tier optimizes for volume over signal, and the numbers everyone compares against are only as good as the care behind the answers. The suppression rules govern how many contributors stand behind a figure and how balanced their shares are; the membership design governs the commitment of the people supplying the data in the first place.

## How benchmark integrity looks for a firm, a vendor, and a consultant

For a firm, the rules mostly surface as confidence at reading time: any comparison that appears has already cleared the floor and the cap, and any cut that has not shows plainly as suppressed. An Elite firm's distribution view — where it sits among peers — appears only when the benchmark behind it publishes under those same rules.

For a vendor, the rules are the terms of anonymous participation. A vendor's data can inform published aggregates, but never as an identifiable voice and never as a dominant one — no cut publishes where any single contributor supplies more than a quarter of the data.

For a consultant or ecosystem owner, the rules hold even at portfolio height. Cross-ecosystem comparison views summarize alignment structure across managed firms, but benchmark cuts inside that picture obey the same floor and cap as everywhere else — an ecosystem view widens what a consultant can see across their own managed firms, not what any benchmark is allowed to reveal.

## A closer look: one scenario, start to finish

Take the third cut from the earlier illustration — four contributors, perfectly balanced, suppressed for want of a fifth — and follow it forward.

A firm in that cohort opens the comparison and sees the cut marked as suppressed. Nothing is broken; the category exists, and the data behind it is simply too thin to publish. The firm's own scores and bands are unaffected — suppression governs what publishes as group context, not what a firm sees of itself.

A quarter later, a fifth firm completes its assessment in that cohort. Only production data counts toward the question — PAT keeps production, pilot, and demo data strictly separated, and demo or pilot data never enters published benchmarks — so the cut's fate turns on real contributors alone. With five balanced contributors, the cut now clears both tests and publishes. The firm that once saw a suppressed marker now sees genuine comparison context, and an Elite member in the cohort can see where it sits within the distribution.

Later still, one contributor leaves PAT. Its data is removed, and the removal is receipted. The aggregates that had already published stay aggregate — individual contributions are not reconstructable from them — and the cut's future publication simply depends, as ever, on whether the remaining data clears the floor and the cap. The bar never moved at any point; the data crossed it.

## Common misconceptions about suppression

"A suppressed cut means something went wrong, or PAT is hiding a bad result." Suppression carries no judgment about anyone in the cut and says nothing about how the group scored. It is purely a statement about the size and balance of the data — the comparison lacks enough balanced contributors to publish, and that is all.

"If the overall contributor pool is large, every comparison will publish." The floor and the cap apply to each cut, not to the dataset as a whole. A large, healthy pool still produces narrow slices with two or three participants, and those slices stay suppressed regardless of the pool around them.

"The 25% cap only matters for tiny groups." The cap binds on its own, at any size. A cut with eight contributors — well above the floor — still suppresses if one of them supplies more than a quarter of the data.

"Someone at PAT decides case by case which cuts to suppress." No one decides anything. The floor and the cap are mechanical rules applied the same way to every cut, every time, for everyone.

## Questions this article answers

**When does PAT publish a benchmark cut?** Only when at least five contributors stand behind it and no single contributor supplies more than 25% of its data. Failing either test keeps the cut from publishing.

**What happens when a cut fails the tests?** It shows as suppressed — a clear indication, not an error, a blank, or a caveated number.

**Why not publish thin cuts with a disclaimer?** Because numbers outlive their disclaimers: a shaky figure travels on without its fine print. Suppression means there is no under-supported number to travel.

**Can a suppressed cut publish later?** Yes. Suppression is a state, not a verdict — when a cut gains contributors or rebalances so that both tests pass, it publishes. The bar never moves; the data clears it.

**Are benchmark contributors identifiable?** No. Contributors are anonymous within benchmarks, and the floor and cap exist partly to keep that anonymity meaningful in practice.

**Does a higher membership tier unlock suppressed cuts?** No. A cut without enough balanced data is suppressed for everyone at every tier; Elite deepens the interpretation of figures that already earned publication.

**Does demo or pilot data feed benchmarks?** Never. Production, pilot, and demo data are strictly separated, and demo or pilot data never enters published benchmarks.

**What happens to a contributor's data if they leave?** It is removed, and the removal is receipted. Previously published aggregates that passed suppression remain aggregate — individual contributions cannot be reconstructed from them.
