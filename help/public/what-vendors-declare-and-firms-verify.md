PAT's model has two sides. Vendors declare, in structured form, what their products are built to do; firms, through assessments of their own operations, supply the evidence of how those capabilities actually perform in practice. Neither side's account stands alone — the two are read together, and the comparison between them is what PAT measures.

## What a vendor declares

On the vendor side, participation begins with a structured declaration of product capabilities. A declaration is not marketing copy restated inside a platform; it is a claim expressed in a structured form, which is what makes it checkable. Prose can flex to fit any evidence after the fact. A structured declaration commits to something specific enough that firm experience can later be held up against it.

This is why the declaration step matters so much to the model. By declaring capabilities in a structured way, a vendor converts its account of its own product from something that can only be believed or doubted into something that can be verified. The vendor is not asked to be modest or to be bold — only to be specific. What it receives in return is alignment evidence: a picture of how its declared capability meets the reality that firms experience.

## Function areas: the shape of a declaration

Vendor declarations are organized across defined function areas of practice software. A vendor does not declare that its product is capable in the round; it declares what the product is built to do area by area, against a defined structure rather than a blank page. That structure is what gives the declaration its granularity, and the granularity is what makes verification specific: firm product assessments measure experienced reality against those declarations within the same function areas, so declaration and experience meet on common ground rather than talking past each other.

The defined structure serves both sides. For the vendor, it removes the temptation and the burden of self-authored framing — the function areas are given, so the declaration becomes a matter of honest placement within a shared map. For the firms whose assessments supply the verification, it means their experience lands against the right claim: what a firm experiences in one area of a product is compared with what was declared for that area, not blended into a single undifferentiated verdict. A product, like a firm, is rarely one thing throughout, and a model that measured alignment only in aggregate would blur exactly the detail both sides most need to see.

## What a firm's assessment verifies

On the firm side, verification is not a separate chore layered on top of the assessment — it is a product of it. Firms in PAT assess their own operations across five pillars, answering structured questions on a consistent numeric scale about how their practice actually runs. Those assessments describe the firm's experienced reality, including its experience of the products in play in its operations.

That is what gives the verification its character. A firm completing an assessment is not filling out a review form about a vendor; it is describing its own operational world, in its own interest, through consistent structured questions. The evidence that reaches the vendor side is a by-product of firms taking their own self-knowledge seriously, which is precisely why it carries weight. Verification borrowed from honest self-assessment is harder to distort than verification solicited as commentary.

The verifying evidence also carries its own quality context. PAT runs response-pattern quality checks — straight-lining detection is one example — and signal-integrity information accompanies scores so that readers know how much weight a result can bear. An integrity flag is information about a response pattern, not an accusation. For the two-sided model, this matters: verification is only as good as the answering behind it, and PAT makes the quality of that answering visible rather than assuming it.

## Where the two sides meet: the alignment delta

The point where declaration and verification touch has a name: the alignment delta, the measured gap between a vendor's declared product capability and firms' assessed experience of it. When the two accounts line up, the delta is small, and the declaration has effectively been confirmed by the field. When they diverge, the delta records the divergence in measured form, rather than leaving it to anecdote or impression — and, importantly, it flows both ways. A product can underperform its declaration, but it can also outperform it: firms may be experiencing more capability than the vendor claimed.

Both directions are findings, not verdicts. The delta is a measurement of alignment between two honest accounts, produced so that each side can see where its picture and the other side's picture differ. The concept deserves its own fuller introduction, and it has one elsewhere in this series; here, the essential point is structural. The delta only exists because the model has two sides. Without structured declarations, there would be nothing to compare experience against; without firm assessments, there would be nothing to compare declarations to.

To make the mechanics concrete, suppose a vendor of practice software declares its capabilities across the defined function areas, claiming particular strength in one of them. Firms using the product complete their assessments in the ordinary way — describing their own operations, including their experienced reality of the products in play. Where the assessed experience in that function area matches the declaration, alignment holds, and the vendor's claim has been confirmed by the field rather than merely repeated by it. Where experience falls short of the declaration, the gap is recorded as a measured divergence tied to that specific area — not a bad review, but a located difference between two structured accounts. And in another function area entirely, the same evidence might show firms experiencing more than the vendor declared: a modestly stated capability outperforming its own description. All three outcomes are the model working as designed.

## What alignment evidence makes possible for a vendor

What a vendor receives from this two-sided model is a structured, evidence-based view of where alignment holds and where it diverges. That is a different asset from anything a vendor can produce about itself. Its usefulness is plainest in honest sales and product conversations: where alignment holds, a vendor can point to evidence grounded in firm assessments rather than restating its own claims more loudly; where alignment diverges, the vendor knows precisely which function areas the divergence lives in, and can bring that knowledge into product conversations as a located finding rather than a vague unease.

The evidence works because of how it was gathered. It came from firms describing their own operations in their own interest — not from solicited testimonials or commentary — so it carries a credibility no self-produced material can match. This is PAT's "evidence, not advertising" philosophy expressed from the vendor's chair: the most persuasive account of a product is the one the vendor did not write.

## Why the displayed numbers trace to their sources

Everything this two-sided model displays follows a single discipline: displayed numbers trace to their sources. PAT calls this evidence lineage, and its rule of thumb is that there are no orphan numbers — no figure appears without a traceable path back to the declarations and assessments that produced it. This is the working expression of PAT's evidence philosophy, "evidence, not advertising": vendors make structured declarations, firm assessments verify them, and what you see on screen is accountable to both.

For a reader, lineage changes what a number is. A figure that cannot say where it came from asks to be trusted; a figure with lineage invites inspection instead. That matters equally on both sides of the model. A vendor looking at alignment evidence about its own product can know that evidence is grounded in actual firm assessments, and a firm reading a comparison can know it was not assembled from assertion. In a model whose whole purpose is holding claims and experience against each other, allowing untraceable numbers anywhere would undermine the exercise — so none are allowed.

The same discipline extends to what the numbers are built from. PAT keeps production, pilot, and demo data strictly separated, so synthetic or trial material never masquerades as field evidence inside the verification picture. Lineage is only meaningful if the sources a number traces back to are themselves the real thing.

## What each side sees

The two-sided model is also role-scoped: each participant sees what its role permits, and no more. Firms see the assessment picture of their own operations. Vendors receive alignment evidence about their own products — how declared capability compares with the assessed experience of firms — rather than a window into any individual firm's internals. Consultants and ecosystem owners, the third role in PAT's world, see the alignment picture across the set of firms they manage.

Within benchmark comparisons, contributors are anonymous, so participating in the verification side of the model does not mean exposing your firm to the vendors whose products you use. The model needs firms' experience in aggregate; it does not need, and does not provide, a way to trace that experience back to a named contributor in a benchmark.

## Where consultants fit in the two-sided model

Consultants and ecosystem owners stand in a third position: they manage ecosystems — a set of member firms plus the products in play across them — and so they see the declare-and-verify model from above rather than from either side. At concept level, their surfaces reflect that vantage: per-firm cards summarizing each firm's alignment status at a glance, structured firm briefs for deeper review of a single firm, and cross-ecosystem comparison views for reading the alignment structure across the whole managed set.

What consultants see is structure and alignment signal — not a firm's private answer text beyond what the role permits. The consultant's value in this model comes from pattern, not surveillance: seeing that several managed firms show the same divergence around the same product capability is actionable structure, and it requires no access to any firm's individual open-ended responses. Role scoping holds here as it holds everywhere in PAT — each of the three roles draws from the shared evidence base exactly what its position justifies, and no more.

## Why the model has two sides at all

It is fair to ask why PAT does not simply ask firms to rate products, or vendors to document features, and stop there. The answer is that either side alone produces a familiar and limited kind of information. Vendor accounts by themselves are declarations without verification — structured or not, they remain one party's view of its own work. Firm experience by itself is verification without a reference point — real, but not anchored to any specific claim, so it cannot say what was confirmed or contradicted.

Put the two together and each side supplies what the other lacks. The declaration gives firm experience something precise to verify; the experience gives the declaration a test it could not administer to itself. Firms gain a product picture grounded in evidence rather than assertion. Vendors gain something genuinely difficult to get any other way: a structured, honest reading of how their product's story holds up in the field — including the welcome cases where the field reports more than the story claimed.

That exchange is the mechanics of PAT in miniature. One side commits to a specific account of capability; the other side, in the course of examining itself, tests that account; and the measured relationship between the two becomes evidence both can use.

## Why PAT works this way

The declare-and-verify model rests on a rule that sounds simple and is demanding in practice: every displayed figure must be able to answer the question "where did you come from." A number whose origin cannot be traced is marketing, whatever screen it appears on, and a model built to hold claims and experience against each other cannot afford a single untraceable figure. Evidence lineage is therefore enforced as a discipline, not offered as a feature: a figure that cannot trace back to the declarations and assessments behind it does not appear.

The role boundaries that keep each side seeing only what its position permits are enforced where enforcement cannot be argued with: in the data layer itself. Security that depends on an assistant's good behavior fails; a permission that lives in the data query cannot be talked out of. That is why firms, vendors, and consultants can all draw on the same shared evidence base without seeing past their role's wall — and why even Ask Pat retrieves only the content the asking user's role permits.

## How this looks for a firm, a vendor, and a consultant

For a firm, the two-sided model asks nothing beyond what the firm is already doing in its own interest: assessing its own operations through structured questions on a consistent scale. The verification the model needs is a by-product of that self-assessment, and the firm's exposure is bounded — benchmark contributions are anonymous, and no vendor receives a window into its individual answers. In return, the firm gains a product picture grounded in evidence rather than assertion.

For a vendor, the model begins with work: declaring product capabilities across defined function areas, specifically enough to be checkable. What comes back is alignment evidence — a structured view of where declared capability and firm-experienced reality hold together and where they diverge, function area by function area, usable in honest sales and product conversations. The vendor never sees which firm contributed what; it sees how its product's story survives contact with the field.

For a consultant managing an ecosystem — a set of member firms plus the products in play across them — the model reads from above. Per-firm cards summarize alignment status at a glance, structured firm briefs support deeper review, and cross-ecosystem comparison views expose the alignment structure across the managed set. The consultant sees structure and alignment signal, not private answer text beyond what the role permits: pattern, not surveillance.

## A closer look: one scenario, start to finish

To carry the earlier sketch through a full arc, give its parties invented names: Fernhill, a practice-software vendor, and Alder & Gray, one of the firms using its product. Fernhill declares its capabilities across the defined function areas, stating particular strength in one — honest placement, area by area, within a shared map.

Alder & Gray, meanwhile, completes its firm assessment for its own reasons: it wants the five-pillar picture of its own operations. In describing that reality, it describes its experience of the products in play, Fernhill's among them — evidence that travels with signal-integrity context, so readers know how much weight it can bear.

When enough firms' assessments accumulate, the alignment picture forms. In the area Fernhill declared as its strength, assessed experience matches the declaration — alignment holds, and Fernhill can point to field-grounded evidence instead of restating its claim more loudly. In a second area, experience falls short; the delta records a located divergence, which Fernhill's product team takes up as a finding rather than a rumor. In a third, the field reports more than was claimed — a modest declaration outperformed by its own product. And a consultant whose ecosystem includes Alder & Gray sees the same divergence recurring across several member firms in the same function area: actionable structure, visible without access to any firm's individual responses. Every displayed number in that chain traces back to the declarations and assessments that produced it.

## Common misconceptions

One misreading is that firms verify vendors by filling out review forms about them. They do not: verification is a by-product of firms assessing their own operations in their own interest, which is why the evidence is harder to distort than solicited commentary.

A second misconception is that the alignment delta only records failures. The delta flows both ways — a product can outperform its declaration as well as underperform it, and outperformance is a finding of the same standing.

A third is that vendors can see which firm reported what. Vendors receive structured alignment evidence about their own products, never any firm's individual answers, and contributors are anonymous within benchmarks.

A fourth is that a declaration is marketing copy hosted in a platform. A declaration is a structured claim, organized across defined function areas and specific enough for firm experience to be held against it — prose can flex to fit any evidence after the fact; a structured declaration cannot.

A fifth is that consultants overseeing an ecosystem can read the firms' answer text. Consultants see structure and alignment signal — cards, briefs, comparisons — not a firm's private responses beyond what the role permits.
