A band in PAT is one of five named levels — Early, Developing, Building, Established, Leading — in which every 0–100 score is presented. Bands lead the presentation and the raw number supports it: when you look at a pillar result, you read the band first and the score beneath it. The mapping from score to band is part of PAT's deterministic scoring machinery, so the same answers always produce the same score and therefore always land in the same band.

## What a band is

A band is a presentation-level construct: a named segment of the 0–100 scale that gives a score a readable meaning. PAT defines exactly five, and they form an ordered lexicon — Early, then Developing, then Building, then Established, then Leading. Every scored result in PAT presents with its band, and the five names are used consistently everywhere scores appear, so "Building" means the same thing on an Integration result as it does on a Governance one.

The relationship between band and number is fixed by design: bands lead, numbers support. The band is the headline reading of a result; the raw score is the supporting detail that tells you where within that reading the result sits. A firm does not receive a band instead of a score or a score instead of a band — it receives both, in that order of emphasis.

Because band assignment is part of the deterministic arithmetic, it inherits all of that arithmetic's properties. No AI model sits anywhere in the path from answers to band. There is no judgment call, no reviewer discretion, and no variation between runs: identical answers produce an identical score and an identical band, every time.

## Why bands lead and numbers support

Bands exist because raw numbers, on their own, invite the wrong kind of reading. A 0–100 scale is precise, and precision can mislead: the difference between 63 and 65 is real arithmetic but rarely a meaningful difference in what a firm should understand about itself or do next. Left alone with raw numbers, readers tend to treat every point as significant and every small gap as a signal. Bands correct for that by stating, up front, the level of resolution at which a result is meant to be read.

The five names also do something numbers cannot: they carry direction. Early, Developing, Building, Established, Leading is a vocabulary of progression — each name describes a stage in a trajectory rather than a grade on an exam. A firm reading "Developing" on its Automation pillar is being told where it is on a path, not being marked down. That framing is deliberate: PAT's assessment exists so firms can see their own operational alignment clearly, and a stage-based lexicon supports that reading better than a bare integer does.

The number still matters, which is why it is never hidden. Within a band, the raw score shows whether a result sits near the lower or upper reaches of its level, and across assessments it lets a firm see movement that has not yet crossed a band boundary. Band first for meaning, number second for resolution — each covers the other's weakness.

## How bands are assigned and read

Band assignment happens at the end of the scoring path. A firm answers its assessment questions on a consistent numeric scale; deterministic arithmetic turns those answers into 0–100 scores (open-ended questions carrying zero weight); and each score presents in the band its value falls within. The five bands partition the scale in a fixed way, applied identically to every firm and every pillar — no cohort-relative adjustment, no grading on a curve. A band reflects where a score sits on the scale, not where a firm sits relative to its peers.

That last distinction is worth holding onto. Peer context in PAT comes from benchmarks, which are a separate construct — comparison context, never rankings, published only under strict suppression rules. A band, by contrast, is absolute: a firm could be the only firm in existence and its 62 would still present in exactly the band the scale assigns to 62.

Bands are read per pillar. A firm does not receive one band overall; it receives a banded result for each of its five pillars, and the shape across them — which pillars sit higher in the lexicon, which sit lower — is often the most informative thing the presentation shows. Signal-integrity information accompanies scores as well, so a reader knows how much weight a banded result can bear; a band presented alongside an integrity flag should be read with that flag in mind, as information about the data rather than as an accusation against anyone.

Movement between bands is the natural way to talk about change. When a firm reassesses and a pillar's score crosses a band boundary, the result now reads one level higher or lower in the lexicon, and that crossing is a more robust statement than any single-point change in the raw number. For Elite members, PAT's deeper interpretive surfaces extend this reading — for example, trajectory over time and position within a distribution — but the band lexicon itself is the same for every member.

## Band movement over time: the intended long-term reading

Band movement across assessments is the reading the whole system is built toward. PAT treats a score as a reading at a point in time, not a permanent label: assessments can be re-taken as operations change, and a firm re-assessing across quarters can see whether an area moved — whether Automation, say, went from Developing to Building. The band lexicon is what makes that cross-time reading trustworthy. Because band assignment is deterministic and the scale's partition never changes, a band crossing between two readings can only mean one thing: the firm's assessed answers changed enough to move the score across a fixed line. Nothing about the measuring stick moved.

Movement runs in both directions, and both directions are information. A pillar that reads one level lower than it did last quarter is not a demotion or a mark against the firm; it is the current reading of an area whose assessed answers have shifted, and it points attention where attention is due. The same point-in-time framing applies upward: crossing into a higher band records where the firm's operations now measure, not a status the platform has conferred or one it guarantees will hold. Each new assessment simply replaces one honest reading with a more recent one, and the sequence of banded readings, pillar by pillar, is the long-term story a firm accumulates.

## Why bands suit a paced cadence

PAT paces engagement deliberately — quarterly rhythms are the kind of cadence it favors — and its reminders are deliberately no-guilt: informative nudges that a fresh reading may be due, never shame mechanics. The band lexicon and that cadence philosophy fit each other. A band is a robust, slow-moving reading: it does not flicker with a point or two of arithmetic, so re-reading it every quarter produces a signal worth the attention it asks for. A firm checking in on a quarterly rhythm is looking for exactly what bands express well — has any area crossed a line since last time — while the raw numbers underneath record the quieter movement between crossings.

This is also why band movement, rather than raw-score movement, is the healthier habit for a firm to build its internal conversation around. A cadence of "did the band move" questions keeps the reading at the resolution the instrument supports, and keeps re-assessment feeling like taking a measurement rather than sitting an exam. The nudge to re-assess is an invitation to update the reading; whatever the new reading says, it is information about now, not a verdict about always.

## Bands across memberships and roles

Bands lead every presentation in PAT, whoever is looking. For firms, Pro membership covers the assessment along with scores, bands, and core insights; Elite adds interpretive surfaces — how scores are trending across assessments, and where a firm sits within a distribution of peers when a benchmark publishes under the suppression rules — but Elite reads the same five-name lexicon as Pro. There is no premium vocabulary: Building means Building at every tier.

The lexicon also serves as shared language beyond the firm's own view. A consultant managing an ecosystem works from per-firm cards summarizing alignment status and from cross-ecosystem comparison views, and banded readings are the vocabulary those surfaces speak, applied identically across every firm in the set — which is what makes a cross-firm view readable at a glance without becoming a league table. And Ask Pat can explain what a banded result means to any signed-in user within their role's scope, answering from PAT's documented content and citing what it draws from; it never generates a score or a band.

## Design rationale — why bands lead, at full depth

The bands-lead-numbers rule is PAT's answer to a specific failure mode of measurement presentation: false precision. A single-point score invites it almost automatically. Print "63" as the headline of a result and readers will treat 63-versus-65 as a finding, rank things by it, set targets against single points of it — not because they are careless, but because a bare number visually claims exactness down to its last digit. The claim is dishonest on the instrument's behalf: an assessment of operational alignment supports readings at the resolution of stages, and a presentation should say so up front rather than leave every reader to rediscover it. Bands are that statement, made structurally. By putting Early, Developing, Building, Established, or Leading first, the presentation declares the level of resolution at which the result is meant to be read before any digit can suggest otherwise.

The design deliberately refuses the opposite failure too. A band-only presentation would discard information the measurement genuinely does support: where within a stage a result sits, and the quiet movement that accumulates between boundary crossings. So the raw number is never hidden — it is demoted, from headline to supporting detail. Bands lead for meaning; numbers support for resolution. The pairing is not a compromise between two presentations but a division of labor in which each half covers the other's characteristic weakness.

The rule's third leg is temporal: movement between bands over time is the reading that matters. A crossing is a robust statement precisely because it does not hinge on a point or two of arithmetic, which makes it the right unit for the long-horizon story a firm accumulates across re-assessments. This is where the rule joins PAT's point-in-time philosophy — operations change, so a permanent label would be false, and re-assessment on a working rhythm is the honest design. A vocabulary built for stage-level readings is the natural language of that rhythm: slow-moving enough that each fresh reading is worth the attention it asks for, expressive enough that a crossing, when it comes, means something.

## A worked example

The firm here is invented and purely illustrative. Suppose Bramwell & Oak, an accounting firm, completes its first assessment and its Automation pillar presents as Developing, with a raw score of 55 in support. The band tells the firm the headline: on automation and AI readiness, it is at an early-to-middle stage of a progression. The number adds resolution: it sits at 55, so within its stage there is a concrete sense of where it stands.

A year of deliberate work later, Bramwell & Oak reassesses. Its Automation score comes back at 64, and suppose that on the scale this now presents as Building. Two things are true at once, and the presentation keeps them in the right order. The band movement — Developing to Building — is the durable statement: the firm has crossed from one stage of the lexicon into the next, and that reading does not hinge on a point or two of arithmetic. The nine-point raw movement is the supporting detail. Had the score instead moved from 55 to 58 without crossing a boundary, the band would have held steady while the number quietly recorded progress — which is exactly the division of labor the design intends.

Follow the firm one reading further. Suppose Bramwell & Oak settles into a quarterly rhythm — prompted by nothing more than PAT's no-guilt nudges — and its next assessment returns Automation at 66. The band holds at Building; the number records two more points of quiet movement within the stage. In the same reading, suppose its Governance pillar comes back a level lower in the lexicon than the quarter before, after real changes in how the firm handles its vendor relationships. That downward crossing is not a mark against the firm. It is a point-in-time reading doing its job: pointing attention at an area whose assessed answers have shifted, in a vocabulary of stages rather than failures.

Across those readings, Bramwell & Oak has begun to accumulate the thing band movement is designed to produce: a per-pillar sequence — Developing, then Building, then Building with a rising number underneath — that tells the firm's operational story at the resolution the instrument supports. Every entry in that sequence came from the same deterministic path, so the story is a series of measurements, comparable end to end.

## What a band is not

A band is not a rank. It never expresses a firm's position relative to other firms; it expresses where a score sits on a fixed scale. Peer comparison lives in benchmarks, which are anonymous context and never league tables.

A band is not a grade, and Early is not a failing mark. The lexicon describes stages of a progression, not passes and failures. Every firm's profile starts somewhere, and the vocabulary is built to make "somewhere" a position on a path.

A band is not a prediction or a promise. Presenting as Established says what the assessed answers measured; it guarantees nothing about future performance, and movement up the lexicon is something a firm does, not something the platform confers.

A band is not an AI output, and it is not a judgment call. No model participates in scoring or band assignment, and no human discretion does either — the same answers produce the same band, always. Ask Pat can help a signed-in user understand what a banded result means within their role's scope, but it never generates scores or bands.

## Common misconceptions

**"Two firms in the same band are performing identically."** A band is a segment of a scale, not a point on it. Two results in Building can sit at opposite reaches of the segment, which is exactly what the supporting raw number is there to show — and a shared band says nothing at all about the two firms' positions relative to each other, because peer context lives in benchmarks, not bands.

**"An unchanged band means nothing happened."** The raw number underneath a steady band can record real movement that has not yet reached a boundary. A band holding while its number climbs is not stagnation; it is the presentation working at its designed resolution while the supporting detail tracks the quieter progress.

**"Leading means the work is finished."** Leading is the highest name in a lexicon of stages, read from a point-in-time measurement. It describes where assessed answers currently land, not a terminal credential — a later assessment produces a fresh reading, and holding the stage is something a firm's operations do, not something the platform confers once and forever.

**"A downward band crossing means the earlier reading was wrong."** Both readings were honest at their moments. PAT treats every score as a reading at a point in time, so a lower band on a later assessment records that the firm's assessed answers changed — an area shifted, and attention is being pointed at it — not that the previous measurement erred.

## Questions this article answers

**What are the five bands, in order?** Early, Developing, Building, Established, Leading — an ordered lexicon used consistently everywhere scores appear.

**Does the band or the number come first?** The band leads and the raw 0–100 score supports it: band first for meaning, number second for resolution within the band.

**How is a band assigned?** Deterministically, at the end of the scoring path: the five bands partition the 0–100 scale in a fixed way, and each score presents in the band its value falls within — same answers, same score, same band, every time.

**Are bands relative to other firms?** No. A band is absolute on the fixed scale, with no cohort-relative adjustment; peer context comes separately from benchmarks, under suppression rules.

**Does a firm receive one overall band?** No. Bands are read per pillar — one banded result for each of the five pillars — and the shape across them is often the most informative part of the presentation.

**What does a band crossing mean?** That the firm's assessed answers changed enough to move the score across a fixed line on an unchanging scale, which makes the crossing a robust statement of real movement.

**Do Pro and Elite members see different bands?** No. The five-name lexicon is identical at every tier; Elite adds interpretive surfaces such as trajectory and distribution position, not a different vocabulary.

**Is a low band a bad grade?** No. The lexicon describes stages of a progression rather than passes and failures, and every reading is a point in time — a position on a path, not a verdict.
