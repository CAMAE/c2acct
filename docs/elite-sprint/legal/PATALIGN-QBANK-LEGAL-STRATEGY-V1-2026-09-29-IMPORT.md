## SECTION A — Objectives, Risk Assessment & Strategic Planning (22: 7E/11M/4H)

**A1 (E)** A 40-lawyer firm's strategic plan states one objective: "grow the firm." Read through the Green Book, the problem with this objective is:
a) it names growth, which internal control standards discourage b) it is not specific or measurable, so progress cannot be assessed consistently c) it was set by partners rather than an oversight body d) it lacks a compliance component — **b.** Principle 6 asks management to define objectives in specific, measurable terms aligned with the entity's mission so performance can be evaluated. *[Green Book 2025, Principle 6, paras. 6.02 to 6.07, adapted to a law firm]*

**A2 (E)** A midsize firm's management committee argues about how much variance in monthly collections is acceptable before someone acts. The Green Book term for what they are trying to define is:
a) a control activity b) a residual risk c) a risk tolerance d) a monitoring baseline — **c.** Principle 6 defines risk tolerance as the acceptable level of variation in performance relative to achieving an objective, set in measurable terms. *[Green Book 2025, Principle 6, paras. 6.08 to 6.10]*

**A3 (E)** A three-attorney firm identifies the risk that its only bookkeeper leaves without notice. The Green Book's set of possible responses to an identified risk is:
a) accept, avoid, reduce or share the risk b) document, escalate or ignore the risk c) insure, litigate or reserve for the risk d) assign, defer or outsource the risk — **a.** Principle 7 describes four risk responses: acceptance within tolerance, avoidance, reduction through controls, and sharing (for example insurance). *[Green Book 2025, Principle 7, paras. 7.13 to 7.14]*

**A4 (M)** A 150-lawyer firm rates the risk of a missed filing deadline "low" because its docketing system catches most errors. A partner objects that the rating should describe the risk before any controls. Under the Green Book the partner is describing:
a) fraud risk b) compliance risk c) tolerance drift d) inherent risk, as distinct from residual risk — **d.** Principle 7 distinguishes inherent risk (absent management response) from residual risk (after response); both are analyzed. *[Green Book 2025, Principle 7, paras. 7.05 to 7.12]*

**A5 (M)** A 25-lawyer firm is replacing its practice management platform. The Green Book principle that asks management to assess how a significant change affects existing risk responses and controls is:
a) Principle 3 (structure and authority) b) Principle 9 (identify, analyze and respond to change) c) Principle 12 (implement control activities) d) Principle 14 (communicate internally) — **b.** Principle 9 covers significant internal changes such as new systems and calls for a change assessment process. *[Green Book 2025, Principle 9, paras. 9.02 to 9.08]*

**A6 (E)** A law firm adopts the Green Book as its planning framework. The most defensible adaptation of the Green Book's terms "management" and "oversight body" to a partnership is:
a) associates and staff b) the managing partner alone and the state bar c) partners with managerial authority and the executive or management committee d) outside counsel and the malpractice carrier — **c.** The Green Book's definition assigns internal control to an oversight body, management and other personnel; in a firm the managing roles sit with partners and the committee that oversees them, consistent with Rule 5.1(a)'s reference to partners and lawyers with comparable managerial authority. *[Green Book 2025, OV1.01; ABA Model Rule 5.1(a); mapping is an adaptation]*

**A7 (M)** A small firm (6 to 20) wants its paralegals and billing staff in the year-end profit-based bonus pool to align incentives with firm results. The Model Rule provision that addresses this arrangement is:
a) Rule 5.4(a)(3), which permits including nonlawyer employees in a compensation or retirement plan based in whole or part on profit sharing b) Rule 5.4(b), which bars any partnership with nonlawyers c) Rule 1.5(e), which governs fee division d) Rule 7.2(b), which governs payment for recommendations — **a.** Rule 5.4(a) lists exceptions to the fee-sharing bar, including nonlawyer employee compensation and retirement plans tied to profit. *[ABA Model Rule 5.4(a)(3)]*

**A8 (M)** A 300-lawyer firm's partner compensation rewards billed hours. Associates report that generative AI cuts drafting time but they still record the old time to hit targets. The ABA opinion that makes this an ethics problem rather than only a compensation problem is:
a) Opinion 08-451 b) Opinion 477R c) Opinion 483 d) Opinion 512, under which an hourly biller may charge only for time actually spent, not time saved — **d.** Opinion 512 applies Rule 1.5 to generative AI: hourly fees reflect time actually spent using the tool and reviewing output. *[ABA Formal Op. 512 (2024), Rule 1.5 discussion]*

**A9 (H)** A midsize firm's risk register lists cyber, trust account and lateral hire risks but records no analysis or outcomes in writing. Under the 2025 Green Book revision the gap is:
a) the register does not name an oversight body b) the results of the risk assessment are not documented, and information security risk is now expressly within Principle 8 c) the register mixes fraud and operational risk d) risks are not ranked by cost — **b.** The 2025 revision requires documentation of risk assessment results and folds information security risk into Principle 8. *[Green Book 2025, Principle 8, paras. 8.12 to 8.19; GAO-25-107721 summary of changes]*

**A10 (M)** A 60-lawyer firm wants an external benchmark for demand growth by practice area (litigation, corporate, real estate, patent) to inform practice-group planning. Of the published surveys, the one that reports practice-level demand for large and midsize firms is:
a) ABA TechReport b) Thomson Reuters Institute / Georgetown State of the Legal Market and the TR Midsize Law Firms report c) Clio Legal Trends d) ILTA Technology Survey — **b.** The TR reports publish practice-level demand growth (for example litigation and patent litigation moving in opposite directions in 2024); the other surveys report technology and small-firm operating metrics. *[TR 2026 State of the US Legal Market p. 6; TR Midsize Law Firms 2025 p. 2; CITED, REPORTED context]*

**A11 (E)** A four-attorney firm of experienced lawyers asks whether it needs a written ethics-referral procedure and a formal planning cycle like a 500-lawyer firm. The Model Rules comment that addresses scaling firm measures to size is:
a) Rule 1.1 Comment 8 b) Rule 1.15 Comment 1 c) Rule 7.1 Comment 2 d) Rule 5.1 Comment 3, which allows a small firm of experienced lawyers to rely on informal supervision and periodic review while a large firm may need more elaborate measures — **d.** Rule 5.1 Comment 3 ties the required measures to firm structure and practice. *[ABA Model Rule 5.1, Comment 3]*

**A12 (M)** A solo practitioner has no technology budget line and decides purchases ad hoc. As a strategy signal, the reported context from the ABA survey is that:
a) most solos have a formal technology budget b) about four in ten solos budget for technology versus about nine in ten firms of 100 or more lawyers, so a budget line is a size-sensitive maturity marker c) budgeting is unrelated to firm size d) solos spend more per lawyer than large firms — **b.** ABA 2024 Solo and Small Firm TechReport reports technology budgets at 41 percent of solos, 55 percent of 2 to 9 firms, 68 percent of 10 to 49 and 90 percent of 100+. *[ABA 2024 Solo and Small Firm TechReport; CITED, REPORTED context]*

**A13 (H)** A 200-lawyer firm's strategic plan asserts that "most peer firms have already re-engineered their work processes." The last Altman Weil survey of firms with 50 or more lawyers reported:
a) only about one in five firms had systematically re-engineered work processes, and median seriousness about changing the delivery model was 5 of 10 b) a majority had re-engineered processes c) nearly all had dedicated project management support d) re-engineering was not measured — **a.** Altman Weil 2020 reported 22 percent had systematically re-engineered processes and 31 percent provided ongoing project management support. *[Altman Weil Law Firms in Transition 2020, executive summary pp. 5 to 6; CITED, REPORTED context, last edition 2020]*

**A14 (M)** A large regional firm's plan adds lawyers in a practice where partners already lack work. The strategic risk the Altman Weil data names most directly is:
a) client concentration b) rate compression c) overcapacity, which half of surveyed firms said dilutes profitability d) lateral attrition — **c.** Altman Weil 2020 reported half of firms saying overcapacity dilutes profit and 84 percent reporting underperforming lawyers. *[Altman Weil Law Firms in Transition 2020, executive summary pp. 5 to 6; CITED, REPORTED context]*

**A15 (M)** A midsize firm performs a risk assessment once a year at the partner retreat and nothing in between. The Green Book expectation this falls short of is:
a) that risk assessment is delegated to an outside auditor b) that risks are assessed only when a loss occurs c) that only compliance risks are assessed d) that risk assessment is both periodic and ongoing as conditions change — **d.** Principle 7 calls for identifying and analyzing risk on a periodic basis and continuously as conditions change. *[Green Book 2025, Principle 7, paras. 7.02 to 7.04]*

**A16 (E)** A firm classifies its objectives. "Every trust account reconciled monthly and certified annually" is, in Green Book terms, primarily:
a) a compliance objective b) an operations objective c) an external reporting objective d) a strategic objective — **a.** Principle 6 groups objectives into operations, reporting and compliance; adherence to laws and rules is a compliance objective. *[Green Book 2025, Principle 6, paras. 6.02 to 6.07]*

**A17 (M)** A 120-lawyer firm's executive committee is asked to state how much risk the firm will accept in pursuing a new contingency practice. In COSO ERM terms, the committee is setting:
a) a control baseline b) risk appetite, within the Strategy and Objective-Setting component c) a portfolio view d) a remediation plan — **b.** COSO ERM 2017 Principle 7 (Defines Risk Appetite) sits in the Strategy and Objective-Setting component. *[COSO ERM 2017, Principle 7; CITED]*

**A18 (H)** A midsize firm's 2026 budget assumes worked-rate growth will match 2025's large-firm rate growth of over 7 percent, while the same report forecasts a slowdown by mid-2026. The Green Book principle that requires the firm to reassess when external conditions change is:
a) Principle 4 b) Principle 11 c) Principle 15 d) Principle 9, which covers external changes such as economic and market conditions — **d.** Principle 9 directs management to identify significant external and internal changes and analyze their effect on risk responses. *[Green Book 2025, Principle 9, paras. 9.02 to 9.04; TR 2026 State of the US Legal Market pp. 3, 7, 18 (REPORTED context)]*

**A19 (M)** A solo and small firm wants to express a risk tolerance for cash flow. It sets "total lockup no more than 90 days, measured monthly." The Green Book view of this statement is:
a) it is a properly quantified tolerance tied to a measurable objective b) tolerances should not be numeric c) lockup is a control activity, not a tolerance d) tolerances apply only to compliance objectives — **a.** Principle 6 asks for tolerances in measurable terms; the 90-day figure sits near the reported small-firm median. *[Green Book 2025, Principle 6, paras. 6.08 to 6.10; Clio Law Firm KPI Benchmarks 2025 (median total lockup 93 days), CITED, REPORTED context]*

**A20 (E)** A firm's strategic plan omits any assessment of the technology it relies on. The Model Rules comment that makes technology awareness part of the duty of competence is:
a) Rule 1.3 Comment 5 b) Rule 1.1 Comment 8, on keeping abreast of the benefits and risks of relevant technology c) Rule 1.6 Comment 19 d) Rule 5.3 Comment 4 — **b.** Comment 8 to Rule 1.1 (2012 amendments) includes the benefits and risks of relevant technology in maintaining competence. *[ABA Model Rule 1.1, Comment 8]*

**A21 (M)** A midsize firm benchmarks its profit-per-lawyer growth since 2019 against the Am Law 100 and concludes it is failing. The reported context that should temper that conclusion is:
a) profit per lawyer is not published by segment b) midsize firms outgrew the Am Law 100 on profit c) the report shows Am Law 100 profit per lawyer grew far faster than midsize since 2019, so the gap is a segment pattern rather than a single firm's failure d) the report covers only solo firms — **c.** TR 2026 reports profit per lawyer since 2019 up 53.7 percent for the Am Law 100, 39.0 percent for the Second Hundred and 25.5 percent for midsize. *[TR 2026 State of the US Legal Market p. 16; CITED, REPORTED context]*

**A22 (H)** A large regional firm's plan assumes corporate clients will keep sending the same volume of routine work. The reported client-side signals that argue for a risk response are:
a) clients plan large spend increases b) clients now require outside counsel to use AI c) clients report no interest in moving work d) about half of general counsel expect work to move in-house over five years, and net spend anticipation has fallen sharply since 2020 — **d.** TR 2026 reports 52 percent of GCs expecting more in-house work and net spend anticipation of 5 percent versus 11 percent in 2020; Green Book Principle 7 asks for a response to identified risks. *[TR 2026 State of the US Legal Market pp. 13, 17 (CITED, REPORTED context); Green Book 2025, Principle 7]*

## SECTION B — Market, Client & Pricing Alignment (24: 7E/12M/5H)

**B1 (E)** A firm sets a fee for a new matter type and asks what the Model Rules require of the amount. The governing standard is:
a) the fee must match the local bar's schedule b) the fee must be below the client's stated budget c) the fee must be reasonable, judged by factors such as time and labor, novelty, skill, customary local charges, results, time limits, relationship, experience and whether the fee is fixed or contingent d) the fee must be hourly — **c.** Rule 1.5(a) prohibits unreasonable fees and lists the factors considered. *[ABA Model Rule 1.5(a)]*

**B2 (E)** A five-attorney firm starts work for a new client and plans to send fee terms "once the matter settles down." The Model Rules timing requirement is:
a) the scope and the basis or rate of the fee are communicated before or within a reasonable time after starting the representation, preferably in writing b) fee terms may be set at the first invoice c) fee terms are required only for contingent matters d) fee terms are required only when the client asks — **a.** Rule 1.5(b) sets the timing and expresses a preference for writing; Rule 1.5(c) makes writing mandatory for contingent fees. *[ABA Model Rule 1.5(b)]*

**B3 (M)** A plaintiff contingency firm's engagement letter states a percentage but not whether expenses are deducted before or after the percentage is applied. The Rule 1.5 element it is missing is:
a) the hourly equivalent b) the client's signature on a waiver of appeal c) a statement of the customary local rate d) the method by which the fee is determined, including whether expenses are deducted before or after the contingent fee is calculated, in a writing signed by the client — **d.** Rule 1.5(c) requires a signed writing stating the method, the percentages for settlement, trial or appeal, and the treatment of expenses, plus a closing statement at the end. *[ABA Model Rule 1.5(c)]*

**B4 (E)** A small firm considers a contingent fee tied to the amount of spousal support obtained in a divorce. Rule 1.5(d) treats this arrangement as:
a) permitted if in writing b) prohibited, alongside contingent fees for defending a criminal case c) permitted if the client is sophisticated d) permitted if the percentage is under one third — **b.** Rule 1.5(d) bars contingent fees contingent on securing a divorce or on alimony or support amounts, and in criminal defense. *[ABA Model Rule 1.5(d)]*

**B5 (M)** A solo refers a personal injury matter to a 30-lawyer firm and asks for one third of the eventual fee without further work. Under Rule 1.5(e), the division is permitted only if:
a) both lawyers are in the same county b) the referring lawyer is paid within 30 days c) the division is proportional to services or each lawyer assumes joint responsibility, the client agrees in writing to the arrangement including the share each receives, and the total fee is reasonable d) the receiving firm discloses the split on its website — **c.** Rule 1.5(e) sets the three conditions for fee division between lawyers in different firms. *[ABA Model Rule 1.5(e)]*

**B6 (M)** A firm charges a fixed fee for a document type it now produces largely with a generative AI tool in minutes. Opinion 512's view of the flat fee is:
a) flat fees are unaffected by the tool used b) flat fees must convert to hourly when AI is used c) the flat fee must be doubled to reflect the tool's cost d) a flat fee that was reasonable before may become unreasonable if the tool makes the work trivial, because Rule 1.5(a) still applies — **d.** Opinion 512 applies the reasonableness standard to flat fees in light of the effort actually required. *[ABA Formal Op. 512 (2024), Rule 1.5 discussion]*

**B7 (M)** A California firm collects a $2,500 flat fee in advance and deposits it to the operating account. The state-specific condition attached to that practice is:
a) the fee must be under $1,000 b) the client must be a business c) the firm must refund half on request d) written disclosure that the client may require trust deposit until earned and is entitled to a refund of any unearned portion, with a signed writing for flat fees over $1,000 — **d.** California Rule 1.15(b) permits advance flat fees in the operating account with those disclosures, a departure from Model Rule 1.15(c). *[Cal. Rules of Prof. Conduct 1.15(b); state-specific]*

**B8 (H)** A midsize firm wants to bill clients for its generative AI tools. Reading Opinion 512 with the DC and Texas opinions, the pricing approach that all three support is:
a) a surcharge for AI-assisted matters set at the firm's discretion b) passing through direct per-use or subscription charges that are disclosed in advance and permitted by the fee agreement, while not billing separately for tools that are ordinary overhead c) billing the time the tool saved d) a profit margin on in-house tools — **b.** Opinion 512 permits direct third-party charges as disclosed expenses and bars billing overhead tools separately; DC 388 and Texas 705 allow out-of-pocket or subscription costs with prior written agreement. *[ABA Formal Op. 512 (2024); D.C. Bar Ethics Op. 388 (2024) and Texas Op. 705 (2025), state-specific]*

**B9 (E)** A firm's website says it "never loses at trial." The Model Rule that governs this statement is:
a) Rule 7.3 b) Rule 1.5 c) Rule 7.1, which bars false or misleading communications about the lawyer's services, including truthful statements that omit facts needed to avoid a materially misleading impression d) Rule 5.5 — **c.** Rule 7.1 covers material misrepresentations and misleading omissions in any communication about services. *[ABA Model Rule 7.1]*

**B10 (M)** A plaintiff firm's advertising lists past verdict amounts without context. The Rule 7.1 comment concern is:
a) creating unjustified expectations that clients will obtain similar results regardless of their facts, which a disclaimer may help prevent b) using numbers in advertising at all c) failing to name the opposing party d) advertising in a medium other than print — **a.** Rule 7.1 Comment 3 treats result advertising as misleading when it suggests similar outcomes without regard to specific facts. *[ABA Model Rule 7.1, Comment 3]*

**B11 (M)** A small firm receives most new matters from a real estate broker and wants to pay the broker a set amount per referral. Rule 7.2 permits:
a) per-referral cash payments if disclosed to the client b) exclusive reciprocal referral agreements c) any payment under $500 d) paying reasonable advertising costs, referral service charges, nominal thank-you gifts not tied to future referrals, and non-exclusive reciprocal referral arrangements disclosed to the client, but not payment for a recommendation — **d.** Rule 7.2(b) bars giving anything of value for recommending the lawyer, subject to listed exceptions. *[ABA Model Rule 7.2(b)]*

**B12 (M)** A firm's business development plan includes emailing accident victims identified in police reports. Rule 7.3 treats a targeted email as:
a) not "live person-to-person contact," so not barred by 7.3(b), though other 7.3 limits (no contact after a request to stop, no coercion or harassment) and Rule 7.1 still apply b) prohibited live solicitation c) permitted only after 30 days d) permitted only to businesses — **a.** The 2018 revision of Rule 7.3 confines the ban to live person-to-person contact and its comments treat written and electronic communications as outside that ban. *[ABA Model Rule 7.3(b), (c) and Comments]*

**B13 (E)** A lawyer's biography says "certified specialist in tax law." ABA Model Rule 7.2(c) requires that the communication:
a) list the lawyer's bar number b) include the number of years practiced c) identify the certifying organization, which must be approved by a state authority or accredited by the ABA d) be reviewed by the state bar before publication — **c.** Rule 7.2(c) allows specialist claims only when certified by an approved or accredited organization that is clearly named. *[ABA Model Rule 7.2(c); state variants differ, e.g. Minnesota Rule 7.2(c) adds a negative disclosure when the certifier is not Board-accredited; state-specific]*

**B14 (M)** A Florida firm advertises that its AI tools produce "better results than any other firm." The state opinion that addresses this claim says:
a) AI claims are exempt from advertising rules b) the claim is acceptable if the firm believes it c) claims that a firm's AI is superior must be objectively verifiable, and unsubstantiated claims violate the advertising rules d) only comparative fee claims are regulated — **c.** Florida Bar Ethics Opinion 24-1 applies the advertising rules to AI capability claims. *[Fla. Bar Ethics Op. 24-1 (2024); state-specific]*

**B15 (E)** A client emails a status question; the responsible lawyer is in trial for two weeks. Rule 1.4 and its comment expect:
a) no response until the lawyer is free b) a response only if the client calls c) a written status report every week d) prompt compliance with the request, or, if not feasible, acknowledgment by the lawyer or staff with a time when a response can be expected — **d.** Rule 1.4(a)(4) and Comment 4 set the promptly-comply-or-acknowledge expectation. *[ABA Model Rule 1.4(a)(4), Comment 4]*

**B16 (M)** A solo and small firm sets a same-day acknowledgment standard for intake calls and emails. The reported market context that supports treating responsiveness as a differentiator is:
a) nearly all firms respond to prospect emails b) only about a third of firms responded to email and fewer than half answered the phone in a mystery-shopper style study c) responsiveness has no relationship to conversion d) the ABA survey reports response times by size — **b.** Clio 2024 reports 33 percent of firms responding to email, 40 percent answering the phone and 48 percent unreachable. *[Clio 2024 Legal Trends Report; CITED, REPORTED context]*

**B17 (M)** A consumer-facing small firm debates offering flat fees for whole matters. The reported client preference and cash-flow context is:
a) clients prefer hourly billing for whole cases b) flat fees slow collections c) most clients prefer flat fees for whole cases, and flat-fee firms bill and are paid sooner d) flat fees are used by fewer than one in ten firms — **c.** Clio 2024 reports 71 percent of clients preferring flat fees for whole cases and flat-fee firms billing and collecting faster. *[Clio 2024 Legal Trends Report; CITED, REPORTED context]*

**B18 (H)** A midsize firm discovers it carries eight lawyer hourly rates with a $400 spread and no written rate policy. The reported context and the governance step it points to are:
a) most midsize firms use one rate, so the firm is an outlier and should consolidate b) multiple rates are typical for midsize firms in the reported data, so the strategic gap is the absence of a documented pricing policy that Green Book Principle 12 would treat as an implementation of control c) rate spreads over $250 are prohibited d) rates are a compliance objective only — **b.** Clio 2025 mid-sized reports 99 percent of mid-sized firms using multiple rates, averaging eight, with a $400 gap; Principle 12 calls for documented policies. *[Clio 2025 Legal Trends for Mid-Sized Law Firms (CITED, REPORTED context); Green Book 2025, Principle 12]*

**B19 (E)** A transactional firm proposes an alternative fee the client has not seen before. The Rule 1.4 duty that governs how much the firm must explain is:
a) Rule 1.4(a)(1), on informed consent notices b) Rule 1.4(a)(3), on status updates c) Rule 1.4(a)(5), on limits on the lawyer's conduct d) Rule 1.4(b), explaining a matter to the extent reasonably necessary to permit the client to make informed decisions — **d.** Rule 1.4(b) requires explanation sufficient for informed decisions, which extends to unfamiliar fee structures. *[ABA Model Rule 1.4(b)]*

**B20 (M)** A firm's marketing says its AI use makes it faster, while its intake survey shows some clients are uneasy about AI. Opinion 512's position on telling clients about AI use is:
a) disclosure is always required in the engagement letter b) disclosure is never required c) disclosure is context dependent, required when the client asks, when engagement terms require it, when consent is needed for confidentiality, when it affects the fee, or when output will influence a significant decision d) disclosure is required only to courts — **c.** Opinion 512 rejects a universal disclosure rule and lists the situations that trigger it; Clio 2025 reports nearly half of clients uncomfortable with lawyer AI use. *[ABA Formal Op. 512 (2024), Rule 1.4 discussion; Clio 2025 Legal Trends Report (CITED, REPORTED context)]*

**B21 (H)** A 500-lawyer firm cites a small-firm survey showing 54 percent of firms bill both hourly and flat to argue hourly billing is fading. The reported large-firm context says:
a) hourly billing still accounts for about 90 percent of legal dollars at large firms, so the two surveys describe different populations and should not be blended b) large firms have moved mostly to flat fees c) neither survey measures billing method d) both surveys report identical figures — **a.** TR 2026 reports hourly billing at about 90 percent of legal dollars; Clio 2025 reports 54 percent of surveyed firms billing both hourly and flat, a mostly small-firm population. *[TR 2026 State of the US Legal Market p. 12; Clio 2025 Legal Trends Report; both CITED, REPORTED context]*

**B22 (M)** A corporate client's outside counsel guidelines require notice before any generative AI is used on its matters. Under Opinion 512 the firm's obligation is:
a) disclosure is discretionary despite the guidelines b) the guidelines are unenforceable c) the firm must disclose only if the AI affects the fee d) disclosure is required because the engagement terms require it — **d.** Opinion 512 lists client engagement terms or outside counsel guidelines requiring disclosure as one trigger for the Rule 1.4 duty. *[ABA Formal Op. 512 (2024), Rule 1.4 discussion]*

**B23 (H)** A firm bills a client for the partner's ten hours spent learning a new AI research tool the firm will use across matters. Opinion 512's position is:
a) the time is billable as research b) the time is billable at half rate c) the time is not billable because learning a tool used regularly is part of basic competence, unless the client specifically asked the lawyer to use an unfamiliar tool and agreed in advance to pay d) the time is billable only to the first client — **c.** Opinion 512 treats learning time as competence, with a narrow exception for client-requested tools agreed in advance. *[ABA Formal Op. 512 (2024), Rules 1.1 and 1.5 discussion]*

**B24 (H)** A midsize firm's strategy names "client demand for AI" as its main driver of technology investment. The reported client-side context is:
a) most general counsel now require outside counsel to use AI b) over 80 percent of general counsel do not require outside counsel to use AI and about 71 percent of corporate clients do not know whether their counsel uses it, so client demand is weak as a present driver c) clients uniformly refuse AI use d) client demand is not surveyed — **b.** TR 2026 reports over 80 percent of GCs not requiring AI use; TR 2025 Gen AI in Professional Services reports 71 percent not knowing. *[TR 2026 State of the US Legal Market p. 13; TR 2025 Generative AI in Professional Services; CITED, REPORTED context]*

## SECTION C — Structure, Ownership & Regulatory Change (20: 6E/10M/4H)

**C1 (E)** A marketing agency proposes to run a firm's intake in exchange for a percentage of resulting fees. The Model Rule provision this arrangement runs into is:
a) Rule 7.3 b) Rule 5.4(a), which bars sharing legal fees with a nonlawyer outside listed exceptions c) Rule 1.5(e) d) Rule 5.5(a) — **b.** Rule 5.4(a) prohibits fee sharing with nonlawyers except in the enumerated cases. *[ABA Model Rule 5.4(a)]*

**C2 (E)** A private equity fund offers to buy a minority stake in a 40-lawyer firm organized as a professional corporation. Under the Model Rule, the provision that addresses nonlawyer ownership of a for-profit law practice is:
a) Rule 1.17 b) Rule 5.1(a) c) Rule 5.4(d), which bars practicing in a for-profit entity where a nonlawyer owns an interest, is a director or officer, or has the right to direct professional judgment d) Rule 7.2(b) — **c.** Rule 5.4(d) restricts nonlawyer ownership and control of a professional practice entity. *[ABA Model Rule 5.4(d)]*

**C3 (M)** A firm's office manager, a nonlawyer, participates in the firm's 401(k) profit-sharing plan. Under Rule 5.4 this is:
a) permitted under the exception for nonlawyer employees in a compensation or retirement plan, even one based on profit sharing b) prohibited as fee sharing c) permitted only if the manager holds no supervisory role d) permitted only in states with alternative business structures — **a.** Rule 5.4(a)(3) expressly allows nonlawyer employees in profit-based compensation or retirement plans. *[ABA Model Rule 5.4(a)(3)]*

**C4 (M)** An Arizona firm plans to take nonlawyer investment. The state-specific framework it must work under is:
a) the ABA Model Rule 5.4 as adopted nationally b) a temporary pilot ending in 2022 c) an exemption limited to accounting firms d) Arizona's elimination of ER 5.4 effective January 1, 2021, with alternative business structures licensed under ACJA 7-209 and required to employ a compliance lawyer who supervises the practice — **d.** The Arizona Supreme Court's August 27, 2020 order removed ER 5.4 and created ABS licensing under ACJA 7-209 effective January 1, 2021. *[Ariz. Sup. Ct. Order R-20-0034 (Aug. 27, 2020); ACJA 7-209; state-specific]*

**C5 (M)** A Utah firm wants to test a nonlawyer-owned service model. The state-specific mechanism is:

**D1 (E)** A 30-lawyer firm rolls out e-signature and wants to know later whether it worked. The Green Book step it needs before rollout is:

**D2 (E)** A firm's IT lead reviews system logs weekly; once a year the firm hires an outside reviewer. In Green Book terms these are:

**D3 (M)** A billing clerk finds that time entries are routinely posted late, tells a paralegal, and nothing changes. The Green Book principle this fails is:

**D4 (M)** A deficiency in matter intake spans three practice groups whose heads disagree on the fix. Under Principle 17 the body expected to direct remediation is:
a) the practice group with the most matters b) the IT department c) the outside auditor d) the oversight body (in a firm, the executive or management committee), which provides direction when deficiencies cross units or involve conflicting interests — **d.** Principle 17 gives the oversight body the role of directing corrective action across units. *[Green Book 2025, Principle 17, paras. 17.06 to 17.08, adapted]*
