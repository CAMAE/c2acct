## SECTION A — Technology Competence and the Duty Framework for Tools (22: 7E/11M/4H)

**A1 (E)** A three-attorney firm asks whether keeping up with practice technology is part of the duty of competence. Under the ABA Model Rules, Comment 8 to Rule 1.1 says a lawyer should:
a) hold a technology certification b) keep abreast of the benefits and risks associated with relevant technology as part of maintaining competence c) delegate all technology decisions to a consultant d) avoid new technology until a bar opinion approves it — **b.** The 2012 Ethics 20/20 amendments folded the benefits and risks of relevant technology into the competence duty. *[ABA Model Rule 1.1, Comment 8]*

**A2 (E)** A 40-lawyer firm is writing its first policy on where client information may be stored. The rule that directly requires reasonable efforts to prevent unauthorized access to or inadvertent disclosure of client information is:
a) Rule 1.4(a) b) Rule 1.15(a) c) Rule 1.6(c) d) Rule 7.1 — **c.** Rule 1.6(c) states the reasonable-efforts duty that every technology choice is measured against. *[ABA Model Rule 1.6(c)]*

**A3 (E)** A small firm (6 to 20) suffered a phishing compromise despite MFA, patching and staff training. Under Comment 18 to Rule 1.6, the fact of unauthorized access:
a) does not by itself constitute a violation if the lawyer made reasonable efforts b) is a per se violation of Rule 1.6 c) is a violation only if the firm lacked cyber insurance d) shifts the burden to the client to prove harm — **a.** Comment 18 says unauthorized access alone is not a violation where reasonable efforts were made. *[ABA Model Rule 1.6, Comment 18]*

**A4 (E)** A solo practitioner sends routine scheduling emails to clients without encryption. ABA Formal Opinion 477R treats unencrypted email for routine communications as:
a) prohibited since 2017 b) permitted only with written client waiver c) permitted only inside a client portal d) generally acceptable if basic, reasonably available security measures are in place — **d.** 477R keeps the 1999 premise for routine communications while requiring a case-by-case analysis for sensitive matters. *[ABA Formal Op. 477R (2017)]*

**A5 (E)** A midsize firm (21 to 100) moves its document management to an internet-based storage provider. The Model Rule comment that names internet-based data storage providers as nonlawyer assistance the firm must make reasonable efforts to supervise is:
a) Rule 1.1, Comment 6 b) Rule 5.3, Comment 3 c) Rule 1.15, Comment 1 d) Rule 5.5, Comment 2 — **b.** Comment 3 to Rule 5.3 lists document management companies and internet-based data storage among outside services subject to the reasonable-efforts duty. *[ABA Model Rule 5.3, Comment 3]*

**A6 (E)** A two-lawyer firm closes its office lease and practices entirely from home offices with cloud tools. ABA Formal Opinion 498 says the Model Rules:
a) permit virtual practice with no physical office provided the usual duties are met b) require at least one physical office for client meetings c) permit virtual practice only for transactional work d) require bar pre-approval of a virtual practice — **a.** Opinion 498 confirms virtual practice is permitted and organizes its risks under competence, confidentiality and supervision. *[ABA Formal Op. 498 (2021)]*

**A7 (E)** A firm administrator asks for the ABA's required list of security controls. Opinion 477R's approach is best described as:
a) a mandatory checklist of firewalls, passwords and encryption b) a rule that every client file must be encrypted c) a process: assess risk, implement responsive measures, verify effectiveness, update continually d) a rule that security is the vendor's responsibility — **c.** 477R declines to prescribe specific measures and instead describes a risk-based process. *[ABA Formal Op. 477R (2017)]*

**A8 (M)** A large regional firm (101 to 500) is scoring a proposed tool against Comment 18 to Rule 1.6. Which of the following is NOT one of the Comment 18 factors for whether efforts are reasonable?
a) sensitivity of the information b) likelihood of disclosure absent additional safeguards c) cost and difficulty of the safeguards d) the vendor's share of the legal market — **d.** The five factors are sensitivity, likelihood of disclosure, cost, difficulty, and adverse effect on the representation; market share is not among them. *[ABA Model Rule 1.6, Comment 18]*

**A9 (M)** A 12-lawyer litigation firm marks outgoing client emails "privileged and confidential." Opinion 477R lists this practice among its practical considerations because it:
a) makes encryption unnecessary b) flags the material for the recipient and helps trigger the recipient's Rule 4.4(b) duties on misdirected information c) satisfies Rule 1.4 disclosure d) is required by the ESIGN Act — **b.** 477R's fifth consideration is labeling client confidential information, which supports Rule 4.4(b) handling by a recipient. *[ABA Formal Op. 477R (2017); ABA Model Rule 4.4(b)]*

**A10 (M)** A transactional firm handles a trade secret matter with highly sensitive technical data. Under Opinion 477R, the lawyer's obligation for communications on that matter is to:
a) inform the client of the risks and discuss whether a more secure channel such as encryption or personal delivery is warranted b) refuse to communicate electronically c) obtain a court protective order before emailing d) route everything through the client's IT department — **a.** 477R says highly sensitive matters call for a discussion with the client about alternative modes; the Comment 18 analysis is repeated case by case. *[ABA Formal Op. 477R (2017)]*

**A11 (M)** A four-lawyer firm of experienced attorneys relies on informal check-ins rather than written supervision procedures for technology use. Comment 3 to Rule 5.1 says:
a) written procedures are mandatory at every firm size b) informal supervision is never sufficient c) a small firm of experienced lawyers may rely on informal supervision and periodic review, while larger firms may need more elaborate measures d) supervision is only required for lawyers under three years of admission — **c.** The comment scales required measures to firm structure and practice. *[ABA Model Rule 5.1, Comment 3]*

**A12 (M)** A midsize firm is deciding how much oversight an outside e-discovery processing vendor needs. Comment 3 to Rule 5.3 says the extent of the obligation depends on:
a) the vendor's price and contract length b) whether the vendor is domestic c) the number of matters the vendor handles d) the nonlawyer's education, experience and reputation, the nature of the services, the terms concerning protection of client information, and the legal and ethical environment of the jurisdiction where services are performed — **d.** Comment 3 uses that factor set to calibrate oversight of outside services. *[ABA Model Rule 5.3, Comment 3]*

**A13 (M)** A solo lawyer conducts client calls from a home office that has a listening-enabled smart speaker. Opinion 498 suggests the lawyer should:
a) obtain written client consent to the device b) consider disabling listening features or not using such devices where client matters are discussed c) treat the device as a Rule 1.6 vendor and run due diligence d) record the calls to preserve an accurate account — **b.** Opinion 498 flags listening-enabled devices as an inadvertent disclosure risk in virtual practice. *[ABA Formal Op. 498 (2021)]*

**A14 (M)** A 60-lawyer firm allows staff to use personal phones for client email. Opinion 498 draws heightened attention to bring-your-own-device arrangements and points to:
a) remote wiping capability and preventing household member access to client information b) a ban on personal devices c) reimbursement of device costs d) a requirement that devices be company-owned within one year — **a.** Opinion 498 names remote wiping and family or household access as the supervision issues in BYOD. *[ABA Formal Op. 498 (2021)]*

**A15 (M)** A plaintiff contingency firm outsources medical record summarization to an offsite provider whose staff will see client health information. Under ABA Formal Opinion 08-451, before the provider accesses Rule 1.6 information the lawyer should:
a) bill the provider's work at a markup b) register the arrangement with the bar c) disclose the arrangement and obtain the client's informed consent d) require the provider to carry malpractice insurance — **c.** 08-451 requires informed consent where outside providers access Rule 1.6 information or where supervision is not close. *[ABA Formal Op. 08-451 (2008)]*

**A16 (M)** A firm completed vendor due diligence on its cloud platform in 2021 and has not revisited it. Opinion 477R's seventh consideration says vendor due diligence should be:
a) done once at contracting b) done only when the client requests it c) replaced by the vendor's marketing security statement d) reassessed periodically — **d.** 477R calls for due diligence on vendors, including cloud providers, reassessed periodically. *[ABA Formal Op. 477R (2017)]*

**A17 (M)** A corporate client's outside counsel guidelines require encryption of all matter communications, beyond what the firm would otherwise use. Under Comment 18 to Rule 1.6, this client instruction:
a) is unenforceable because the rule sets the ceiling b) is permitted; a client may require special security measures not otherwise required, and may also give informed consent to forgo measures the rule would otherwise require c) requires bar approval d) converts the firm into a financial institution — **b.** Comment 18 allows clients to require more or consent to less. *[ABA Model Rule 1.6, Comment 18]*

**A18 (M)** A small firm asks what ABA Formal Opinion 483 expects of it before any breach happens. The opinion:
a) expects reasonable efforts to monitor for breaches and recommends an incident response plan with assigned roles b) requires a third-party penetration test each year c) requires notifying all clients of the firm's security posture d) requires cyber insurance — **a.** 483 reads Rules 1.1, 1.6(c), 5.1 and 5.3 to require monitoring and recommends a proactive incident response plan. *[ABA Formal Op. 483 (2018)]*

**A19 (H)** After a breach of its document system, a 200-lawyer firm asks which clients it must notify as a matter of ethics. Opinion 483:
a) requires notice to all current and former clients b) requires notice to no one until law enforcement approves c) requires notice to current clients whose material confidential information was or is reasonably suspected to be compromised, and declines to require notice to former clients as a matter of ethics d) leaves notice entirely to state breach statutes — **c.** 483 grounds current-client notice in Rule 1.4 and separately reminds firms that statutory breach laws apply in parallel. *[ABA Formal Op. 483 (2018); ABA Model Rule 1.4]*

**A20 (H)** A client insists the firm use the client's chosen e-discovery vendor. Comment 4 to Rule 5.3 says the lawyer should ordinarily:
a) decline the representation b) treat the vendor as fully supervised by the client c) accept without further steps because the client chose it d) agree with the client on how responsibility for monitoring the vendor is allocated, noting matters before a tribunal may carry additional obligations — **d.** Comment 4 addresses client-directed providers and points to Rule 1.2 allocation. *[ABA Model Rule 5.3, Comment 4; ABA Model Rule 1.2]*

**A21 (H)** A firm asks whether every client communication channel needs special security. Comment 19 to Rule 1.6 says:
a) special measures are required for every electronic communication b) special measures are not required where the method affords a reasonable expectation of privacy, but special circumstances, including other law such as privacy statutes, may warrant more c) only encrypted channels afford a reasonable expectation of privacy d) the client's consent removes the duty entirely — **b.** Comment 19 sets the baseline and reserves room for other law and special circumstances. *[ABA Model Rule 1.6, Comment 19]*

**A22 (H)** Ransomware encrypts a firm's file server for four days; no exfiltration is found. Under Opinion 483's definition, the episode can still be a "data breach" for ethics purposes because the definition includes:
a) an episode that significantly impairs the lawyer's ability to perform the legal services for which the lawyer was hired b) any malware detection c) any event reported to insurance d) only events involving misappropriation of data — **a.** 483's definition has two prongs: compromise of material client confidential information, or significant impairment of the lawyer's ability to perform services. *[ABA Formal Op. 483 (2018)]*

## SECTION B — Generative AI Use, Disclosure, Verification and Billing (26: 8E/13M/5H)

**B1 (E)** A managing partner asks whether the ABA wrote new rules for generative AI. ABA Formal Opinion 512 (July 29, 2024):
a) creates a new Model Rule 1.19 on AI b) suspends Rule 1.6 for AI tools c) applies existing Rules 1.1, 1.4, 1.5, 1.6, 3.1, 3.3, 5.1, 5.3 and 8.4 to generative AI tools d) bans generative AI in court filings — **c.** 512 is an application of existing rules, not a new rule. *[ABA Formal Op. 512 (2024)]*

**B2 (E)** An associate at a small firm asks how much she must know about a generative AI tool before using it. Opinion 512 says the lawyer:
a) must be able to explain the model architecture b) needs no understanding if the vendor is reputable c) must hold a technology CLE certificate d) must have a reasonable understanding of the capabilities and limitations of the specific tool, maintained through self study, CLE or consulting others — **d.** 512 sets a reasonable-understanding standard, not an expert standard. *[ABA Formal Op. 512 (2024); ABA Model Rule 1.1]*

**B3 (E)** A litigation associate pastes case citations from a generative AI research summary into a brief. Opinion 512 says the lawyer must:
a) independently verify the output, including citations, before filing b) file and correct later if opposing counsel objects c) rely on the tool if it is legal-specific d) disclose the tool in a footnote instead of verifying — **a.** 512 notes tools have produced citations to nonexistent opinions and requires review before filing. *[ABA Formal Op. 512 (2024); ABA Model Rule 3.3]*

**B4 (E)** A generative AI tool cuts a three-hour drafting task to one hour. For an hourly client, Opinion 512 says the lawyer may bill:
a) the three hours the task would have taken b) only the time actually spent using the tool and reviewing the output c) three hours if the client is told AI was used d) a flat premium for efficiency — **b.** Hourly billers may not charge for time saved. *[ABA Formal Op. 512 (2024); ABA Model Rule 1.5]*

**B5 (E)** A firm's technology committee wants a framework for managing AI risk. NIST AI RMF 1.0 organizes its core into four functions:
a) Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond b) Plan, Do, Check, Act c) Govern, Map, Measure, Manage d) Design, Build, Test, Deploy — **c.** AI RMF 1.0 (NIST AI 100-1) uses Govern, Map, Measure and Manage. *[NIST AI 100-1, AI RMF 1.0 (2023)]*

**B6 (E)** A 600-lawyer firm's general counsel asks whether NIST AI RMF 1.0 is mandatory for law firms. The framework describes itself as:
a) binding on federal contractors only b) binding on any organization over 500 employees c) a certification standard d) voluntary, rights-preserving, non-sector-specific and use-case agnostic — **d.** NIST states the framework is intended to be voluntary. *[NIST AI 100-1, AI RMF 1.0 (2023)]*

**B7 (E)** A lawyer wants to upload a client's contract to a generative AI tool that learns from user inputs. Opinion 512 says that before inputting information relating to the representation into a self-learning tool the lawyer must:
a) obtain the client's informed consent in advance b) redact the client name only c) rely on the tool's privacy policy d) notify the bar — **a.** Self-learning tools may surface one client's information to other users, so advance informed consent is required. *[ABA Formal Op. 512 (2024); ABA Model Rule 1.6]*

**B8 (E)** A partner spends four hours learning a generative AI tool the firm will use routinely. Opinion 512 says that time:
a) may be billed to the first client who benefits b) may not be billed to a client, because learning a regularly used tool is basic competence c) may be billed at half rate d) may be billed as an expense — **b.** Training time on a tool used regularly is not billable, with a narrow exception where a client requests a specific tool and agrees in advance to pay. *[ABA Formal Op. 512 (2024); ABA Model Rule 1.5]*

**B9 (M)** A firm adds a sentence to every engagement letter stating that the firm may use AI tools. For a self-learning tool, Opinion 512 treats that clause as:
a) sufficient informed consent b) sufficient if the client initials it c) sufficient for transactional matters only d) insufficient; the client must be told why the tool is used, the specific risks, what could be disclosed and how others could use it — **d.** 512 rejects general boilerplate as informed consent. *[ABA Formal Op. 512 (2024); ABA Model Rule 1.0(e)]*

**B10 (M)** A midsize firm asks whether it must tell every client whenever generative AI touches a matter. Opinion 512 says disclosure under Rule 1.4 is:
a) always required b) never required c) context dependent, and required when the client asks, when engagement terms or outside counsel guidelines require it, when consent is needed, when the fee is affected, or when output will influence a significant decision d) required only in litigation — **c.** 512 sets no universal disclosure rule and lists the triggers. *[ABA Formal Op. 512 (2024); ABA Model Rule 1.4]*

**B11 (M)** A lawyer uses a public generative AI tool to brainstorm general deposition topics without entering any facts about the client or matter. Under Opinion 512, client consent is:
a) not needed, because no information relating to a representation is input b) needed because the tool is public c) needed for any litigation use d) needed unless the tool is legal-specific — **a.** 512 excepts idea generation that involves no information relating to the representation. *[ABA Formal Op. 512 (2024)]*

**B12 (M)** A firm's word processor includes an AI grammar and style checker integrated into routine drafting. Opinion 512 treats charges for tools of this kind as:
a) billable per document b) overhead that cannot be billed separately to clients c) billable only in flat fee matters d) billable if disclosed in the engagement letter — **b.** 512 gives grammar checking software as its example of an overhead tool that cannot be billed separately. *[ABA Formal Op. 512 (2024); ABA Model Rule 1.5]*

**B13 (M)** A 150-lawyer firm has no written position on generative AI. Under Opinion 512 the managerial lawyers' obligation under Rules 5.1 and 5.3 is to:
a) prohibit all generative AI use b) let each practice group decide informally c) establish clear policies on permissible use, train lawyers and staff on capabilities, limits, ethics and secure data handling, and monitor compliance d) rely on the vendor's terms of use as the policy — **c.** 512 places policy, training and monitoring on managerial and supervisory lawyers. *[ABA Formal Op. 512 (2024); ABA Model Rules 5.1, 5.3]*

**B14 (M)** Before contracting with a third-party generative AI vendor, Opinion 512 suggests the firm examine which of the following?
a) the vendor's advertising claims b) the vendor's number of law firm customers c) the vendor's pricing tiers only d) the vendor's security policies, confidentiality agreements, breach notification rights, conflicts checking, data retention and ownership after the engagement, and available remedies — **d.** 512 repeats the vendor diligence factors that 477R and 08-451 established. *[ABA Formal Op. 512 (2024); ABA Formal Op. 477R (2017)]*

**B15 (M)** A Florida firm plans to send client documents to a third-party generative AI service. Florida Bar Ethics Opinion 24-1 says the lawyer must first:
a) obtain the client's informed consent before disclosing confidential information to the third-party service b) file a notice with the Florida Bar c) confirm the vendor is headquartered in the United States d) nothing beyond ordinary vendor diligence — **a.** Florida's opinion analogizes to its cloud opinion and requires informed consent for third-party AI; it treats adequately secured in-house AI as lower risk. *[Florida Bar Ethics Op. 24-1 (2024); state-specific]*

**B16 (M)** A California firm is vetting a generative AI tool for use on client matters. The State Bar of California's 2023 Practical Guidance says the lawyer should:
a) rely on the vendor's marketing security page b) review the terms of use to learn whether inputs are used for training or shared, anonymize where possible, and avoid tools that lack adequate confidentiality protections c) obtain a court order before any use d) use only tools listed by the State Bar — **b.** California's guidance keys confidentiality on the tool's training and sharing practices and on anonymization. *[State Bar of California, Practical Guidance for the Use of Generative AI (2023); state-specific]*

**B17 (M)** A North Carolina lawyer uses generative AI for routine legal research and case management. Under North Carolina 2024 Formal Ethics Opinion 1, client disclosure is:
a) required for every use b) required only for litigation c) required only if the client is a business d) not required for routine uses, but advance informed consent is required when substantive work is delegated to AI in a manner analogous to outsourcing to nonlawyers, or when the use affects fees — **d.** North Carolina draws the line at delegation of substantive work and fee impact. *[N.C. State Bar 2024 Formal Ethics Op. 1 (2024); state-specific]*

**B18 (M)** A firm cannot say which of its systems now include generative AI features. The NIST AI RMF 1.0 subcategory that calls for mechanisms to inventory AI systems sits in the:
a) Map function b) Measure function c) Govern function (Govern 1.6) d) Manage function — **c.** Govern 1.6 calls for mechanisms to inventory AI systems and resource them according to risk. *[NIST AI 100-1, AI RMF 1.0, Govern 1.6 (2023)]*

**B19 (M)** A midsize firm has not written down what a contract review tool is for, who will use it, or what harm a wrong answer could cause. In NIST AI RMF 1.0 terms, the function that establishes context, intended purpose and potential impacts is:
a) Map b) Govern c) Measure d) Manage — **a.** Map establishes the context to frame risks related to an AI system. *[NIST AI 100-1, AI RMF 1.0, Map (2023)]*

**B20 (M)** A litigation group wants to know how often an AI summarization tool misstates a deposition. Under NIST AI RMF 1.0, tracking that error rate with defined tests is the:
a) Govern function b) Measure function, supporting the "valid and reliable" trustworthiness characteristic c) Map function d) Manage function — **b.** Measure employs quantitative and qualitative methods to analyze, assess, benchmark and monitor AI risk; validity and reliability are the first trustworthiness characteristic. *[NIST AI 100-1, AI RMF 1.0, Measure (2023)]*

**B21 (M)** A firm wants a vendor-neutral name for the risk that a generative AI tool produces confident but false content. The NIST Generative AI Profile calls this:
a) drift b) overfitting c) latency d) confabulation — **d.** NIST AI 600-1 lists confabulation among twelve risks unique to or exacerbated by generative AI. *[NIST AI 600-1, Generative AI Profile (2024)]*

**B22 (H)** A firm charges a fixed fee for a standard document that generative AI now produces in minutes with minimal review. Opinion 512 warns that:
a) flat fees are exempt from Rule 1.5 b) the fee is reasonable as long as it was reasonable when set c) a flat fee that was reasonable before may become unreasonable if the tool makes the work trivial d) flat fees must be converted to hourly — **c.** 512 applies Rule 1.5 reasonableness to flat fees after AI changes the effort. *[ABA Formal Op. 512 (2024); ABA Model Rule 1.5]*

**B23 (H)** A firm with offices in the District of Columbia and Florida is writing one client disclosure policy for generative AI. Comparing the two jurisdictions' guidance:
a) D.C. Opinion 388 says blanket disclosure of generative AI use is not required, while Florida 24-1 recommends informed consent before confidential information goes to a third-party service, so the policy should meet the more demanding trigger where it applies b) both require disclosure in every matter c) both treat AI use as never requiring disclosure d) D.C. is stricter than Florida on consent — **a.** The two opinions sit at different points on the disclosure spectrum, but both expect consent before client confidential information goes to a third-party generative AI tool, and both are state-specific. *[D.C. Bar Ethics Op. 388 (2024); Florida Bar Ethics Op. 24-1 (2024); both state-specific]*

**B24 (H)** A 300-lawyer firm built its own internal generative AI tool and wants to recover the cost from clients. Opinion 512 permits:
a) billing a per-use rate set to earn a margin b) billing direct cost plus a reasonable overhead allocation, disclosed in advance, but not as a profit center c) no recovery of any kind d) recovery only through hourly rates — **b.** 512 distinguishes pass-through of direct third-party charges, in-house tools at cost plus overhead, and prohibited profit centers. *[ABA Formal Op. 512 (2024); ABA Model Rule 1.5]*

**B25 (H)** A Florida plaintiff firm deploys a website chatbot to screen potential clients. Florida Opinion 24-1 says the chatbot must:
a) be reviewed by the Florida Bar before launch b) collect a signed engagement before answering questions c) be limited to office hours d) disclose that it is not a lawyer and be designed to avoid inadvertently forming an attorney-client relationship or giving legal advice — **d.** Florida's opinion addresses client-facing AI intake directly. *[Florida Bar Ethics Op. 24-1 (2024); state-specific]*

**B26 (H)** After filing, an associate learns a brief contained a citation the AI tool fabricated. Opinion 512, reading Rules 3.3 and 8.4(c), points to the duty to:
a) wait for the court to notice b) withdraw from the matter c) correct the inaccurate citation and analysis with the tribunal d) disclose the tool's name to opposing counsel only — **c.** 512 ties verification and correction of AI output to candor toward the tribunal and the bar on dishonesty. *[ABA Formal Op. 512 (2024); ABA Model Rules 3.3, 8.4(c)]*

## SECTION C — Automation in Discovery, Document Review and Drafting (22: 6E/11M/5H)

**C1 (E)** A litigation firm automates production of discovery responses from a template. Under FRCP 26(g), the attorney's signature on each response certifies that:
a) the client has read it b) it is warranted by law, not interposed for an improper purpose, and neither unreasonable nor unduly burdensome given the needs of the case c) all documents have been reviewed by a lawyer d) the response was generated without software — **b.** Rule 26(g)(1) makes the signing lawyer accountable for the reasonableness of the response and the effort behind it. *[Fed. R. Civ. P. 26(g)(1)]*

**C2 (E)** Opposing counsel demands every email from 40 custodians in a $90,000 contract dispute. The six proportionality factors a court weighs under FRCP 26(b)(1) include:
a) importance of the issues, amount in controversy, relative access to information, parties' resources, importance of the discovery, and whether burden outweighs benefit b) the number of custodians, the age of the data, and the vendor's fee c) the size of the law firm and its technology budget d) whether the requesting party used technology-assisted review — **a.** Rule 26(b)(1) lists those six factors. *[Fed. R. Civ. P. 26(b)(1)]*

**C3 (E)** A firm using automated privilege screening wants protection against waiver in later state court proceedings. The instrument that makes a non-waiver ruling binding in all other federal and state proceedings is:
a) a private clawback agreement between counsel b) a Rule 26(f) discovery plan c) a protective order under Rule 26(c) d) a court order under FRE 502(d) — **d.** 502(d) orders reach other proceedings; 502(e) agreements bind only the parties unless incorporated into an order. *[Fed. R. Evid. 502(d), 502(e)]*

**C4 (E)** A requesting party insists the responding firm use a specific review workflow. Sedona Principle 6 states that:
a) the requesting party sets the method b) the court sets the method in every case c) responding parties are best situated to evaluate the procedures, methodologies and technologies for preserving and producing their own ESI d) both sides must use identical tools — **c.** Principle 6 places method selection with the responding party, subject to the Principle 7 burden on a motion to compel. *[The Sedona Principles, Third Edition (2018), Principle 6; CITED]*

**C5 (E)** A midsize firm prefers keyword search over technology-assisted review on a matter. According to the Sedona TAR Case Law Primer, courts have generally:
a) ordered TAR whenever it is more accurate b) declined to compel a party to use TAR against its preference, absent serious discovery breakdowns c) required TAR in all federal cases since 2015 d) banned keyword search — **b.** The Primer reports that reasonableness, not the best tool, satisfies discovery obligations, with narrow exceptions. *[Sedona Conference TAR Case Law Primer, Second Edition (2023); CITED]*

**C6 (E)** A firm's email system auto-deletes messages after 90 days. When litigation becomes reasonably foreseeable, the 2015 Committee Note to FRCP 37(e) expects a party to:
a) intervene in the routine operation of its information systems to prevent loss of relevant ESI b) keep auto-deletion running until a court orders otherwise c) delete only privileged material d) migrate all data to a vendor — **a.** The Note describes the litigation hold as intervention in routine deletion once litigation is reasonably anticipated. *[Fed. R. Civ. P. 37(e), 2015 Committee Note]*

**C7 (M)** A firm's automated response tool served objections that were boilerplate and unreasonable, without substantial justification. Under FRCP 26(g)(3), the court:
a) may issue a warning only b) may sanction only the client c) has no authority absent a motion d) must impose an appropriate sanction on the signer, the party, or both — **d.** Rule 26(g)(3) is mandatory: the court must impose an appropriate sanction for an unjustified certification violation. *[Fed. R. Civ. P. 26(g)(3)]*

**C8 (M)** A firm produced a privileged memo by mistake after running an automated privilege screen. To avoid waiver under FRE 502(b), the firm must show:
a) that the screen was vendor-certified b) that opposing counsel acted in bad faith c) that the disclosure was inadvertent, that reasonable steps were taken to prevent it, and that prompt reasonable steps were taken to rectify it d) that the memo was marked privileged — **c.** 502(b) has three elements; the Committee Note adds that use of advanced analytical software in screening may support the reasonable-steps finding. *[Fed. R. Evid. 502(b) and Committee Note]*

**C9 (M)** A firm wants to cull a large collection with search terms and then run technology-assisted review on what remains. The Sedona TAR Case Law Primer reports that courts are:
a) unanimous that this is prohibited b) divided, with most recent decisions permitting TAR after keyword culling as proportional and a minority requiring TAR on the full set c) unanimous that culling is required first d) silent on the question — **b.** The Primer describes a split, with proportionality-focused courts accepting layered approaches. *[Sedona Conference TAR Case Law Primer, Second Edition (2023); CITED]*

**C10 (M)** Opposing counsel asks for the firm's TAR training documents and validation metrics before any deficiency in production is shown. Per the Sedona TAR Case Law Primer, courts have generally:
