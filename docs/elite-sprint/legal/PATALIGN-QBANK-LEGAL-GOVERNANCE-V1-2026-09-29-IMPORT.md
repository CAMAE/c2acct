## SECTION A — Firm Governance, Supervision & Control Environment (22: 7E/11M/4H)

**A1 (E)** The three partners of a 14-lawyer firm say ethics compliance is "each lawyer's own job" and keep no firm-level procedures. Under the Model Rules, the partners' own duty is to:
a) supervise only the associates they personally assign b) make reasonable efforts to ensure the firm has measures giving reasonable assurance that all its lawyers conform to the Rules c) obtain malpractice insurance in place of written procedures d) certify each lawyer's compliance annually to the bar — **b.** Rule 5.1(a) places a systems-level duty on partners and lawyers with comparable managerial authority, not just on direct supervisors. *[ABA Model Rule 5.1(a)]*

**A2 (E)** A midsize firm is drafting its first written policy set. The Comment to Rule 5.1 names, as examples of measures a firm's procedures should cover, all of the following except:
a) detecting and resolving conflicts of interest b) identifying dates by which actions must be taken c) setting partner compensation formulas d) accounting for client funds and property and supervising inexperienced lawyers — **c.** Comment 2 to Rule 5.1 lists conflicts, deadlines, client funds and supervision of inexperienced lawyers; compensation is not among them. *[ABA Model Rule 5.1, Comment 2]*

**A3 (E)** A solo practitioner assumes Rule 5.3 covers only her one employee. Since the 2012 amendments, Rule 5.3's title and comments make clear the rule reaches:
a) nonlawyer assistance generally, including outside services such as document management and internet-based data storage b) only employees on the firm's payroll c) only nonlawyers who work on site d) only nonlawyers licensed as paralegals — **a.** The 2012 retitling from "Assistants" to "Assistance" and Comment 3 extend the rule to outside nonlawyer services. *[ABA Model Rule 5.3, Comment 3]*

**A4 (M)** A four-attorney firm of experienced lawyers asks whether it needs the same ethics infrastructure as a 400-lawyer firm. The Comment to Rule 5.1 says:
a) every firm needs a written ethics manual and a compliance officer b) firms under ten lawyers are exempt from Rule 5.1 c) the measures are identical regardless of size d) required measures depend on firm structure and practice; a small firm of experienced lawyers may rely on informal supervision and periodic review, while a large firm may need more elaborate measures — **d.** Comment 3 to Rule 5.1 scales the required measures to the firm's structure and the nature of its practice. *[ABA Model Rule 5.1, Comment 3]*

**A5 (M)** A department head at a large regional firm learns that an associate missed a filing deadline that can still be cured by motion, and does nothing. Under Rule 5.1(c), the department head:
a) has no exposure because he did not order the conduct b) may be responsible for the violation because he knew of it at a time when its consequences could be avoided or mitigated and failed to take reasonable remedial action c) is responsible only if the client complains d) is responsible only if he directly supervised the associate on that matter — **b.** Rule 5.1(c)(2) attaches responsibility to partners and managers who know of a violation when it can still be remedied and fail to act. *[ABA Model Rule 5.1(c)(2)]*

**A6 (E)** A second-year associate follows a supervising partner's resolution of an arguable question of professional duty, and the resolution later proves debatable. Under Rule 5.2, the associate:
a) is fully responsible because she should have researched the question independently b) is responsible only if the client is harmed c) does not violate the Rules by acting in accordance with a supervisor's reasonable resolution of an arguable question d) is never responsible for conduct taken at a supervisor's direction — **c.** Rule 5.2(b) protects a subordinate who acts on a supervisor's reasonable resolution of an arguable question; Rule 5.2(a) otherwise keeps the subordinate bound. *[ABA Model Rule 5.2(a) and (b)]*

**A7 (M)** A small firm hires a receptionist with no legal background and gives no orientation on client confidentiality. The Comment to Rule 5.3 says nonlawyer assistants must be given:
a) appropriate instruction and supervision on the ethical aspects of their work, especially confidentiality, taking into account that they lack legal training and are not subject to professional discipline b) a copy of the Rules and a signed acknowledgment c) the same CLE as the lawyers d) supervision only when they handle client funds — **a.** Comment 2 to Rule 5.3 keys the duty to the assistant's lack of legal training and the absence of disciplinary exposure. *[ABA Model Rule 5.3, Comment 2]*

**A8 (E)** A managing partner at a 60-lawyer firm says technology decisions are "an IT matter, not an ethics matter." The Comment to Rule 1.1 on maintaining competence says a lawyer should keep abreast of changes in the law and its practice, including:
a) only changes in court e-filing rules b) only technology the lawyer personally operates c) technology only when a client requests it d) the benefits and risks associated with relevant technology — **d.** Comment 8 to Rule 1.1, added in 2012, folds relevant technology into the duty of competence. *[ABA Model Rule 1.1, Comment 8]*

**A9 (M)** A midsize firm has a conflicts database, a docketing system and a trust ledger, but no one is named as owner of any of them. Read for a law firm, the Green Book principle most directly on point is:
a) Principle 13, use quality information b) Principle 3, establish structure, responsibility and authority, so that each control has an assigned owner c) Principle 15, communicate externally d) Principle 6, define objectives and risk tolerances — **b.** Principle 3 assigns responsibility and delegates authority for internal control; unowned controls are a Principle 3 gap. *[GAO Green Book 2025, Principle 3]*

**A10 (M)** A large regional firm has written policies on conflicts and docketing but no consequence has ever followed a lapse. The Green Book component this weakens is the control environment, specifically the principle that management:
a) documents risk assessment results b) prioritizes preventive controls c) enforces accountability for internal control responsibilities d) communicates with external parties — **c.** Principle 5, enforce accountability, holds personnel accountable for their control responsibilities; policies without consequences fail that principle. *[GAO Green Book 2025, Principle 5]*

**A11 (H)** A firm's risk committee debates whether to invest in a pre-disbursement approval step or a monthly after-the-fact review of trust disbursements. The 2025 Green Book revision added language stating that management:
a) prioritizes preventive control activities where appropriate b) treats detective and preventive controls as interchangeable c) relies on detective controls where staff are experienced d) uses preventive controls only for information technology — **a.** The 2025 revision's overview (OV4.16) adds an express preference for preventive control activities where appropriate. *[GAO Green Book 2025, OV4.16]*

**A12 (M)** A partner instructs a paralegal to sign a client's name to a settlement release "to save time." Beyond any other rule, Rule 8.4(a) treats the partner's instruction as:
a) a supervision matter under Rule 5.3 only b) misconduct only if the release is later challenged c) permissible because the client authorized settlement d) misconduct, because a lawyer may not knowingly assist or induce another to violate the Rules or do so through the acts of another — **d.** Rule 8.4(a) reaches violations accomplished through another person's acts. *[ABA Model Rule 8.4(a)]*

**A13 (M)** An associate at a small firm revises a time entry after the fact to describe work that was not performed, and the entry goes to the client on an invoice. The Rule 8.4 paragraph most directly implicated is:
a) 8.4(d), conduct prejudicial to the administration of justice b) 8.4(c), conduct involving dishonesty, fraud, deceit or misrepresentation c) 8.4(b), a criminal act reflecting on fitness d) 8.4(a), violating the Rules through another — **b.** A knowingly false billing description sent to a client is a misrepresentation under Rule 8.4(c); other paragraphs may also apply but 8.4(c) is the direct fit. *[ABA Model Rule 8.4(c)]*

**A14 (E)** A three-attorney firm runs no conflicts check at intake and later discovers it is adverse to an existing client. The Comment to Rule 1.7 says that ignorance of a conflict caused by failure to have identification procedures:
a) is a complete defense for firms under five lawyers b) shifts responsibility to the client who did not disclose c) does not excuse the violation; a firm should adopt reasonable procedures appropriate to its size and type of practice d) is excused if the firm relied on memory in good faith — **c.** Comment 3 to Rule 1.7 requires reasonable identification procedures scaled to the firm and denies an ignorance defense. *[ABA Model Rule 1.7, Comment 3]*

**A15 (M)** A client gives oral consent to a concurrent conflict during a phone call late on a Friday. Under the Comment to Rule 1.7 on "confirmed in writing," the lawyer:
a) may record and transmit the writing within a reasonable time thereafter if it was not feasible to do so at the time consent was given b) must obtain a client-signed document before any work continues c) may rely on the oral consent indefinitely if a file note is made d) must obtain the client's signature within 24 hours — **a.** Comment 20 to Rule 1.7 allows a lawyer-generated writing promptly transmitted, with a reasonable-time backstop when immediate transmission is not feasible. *[ABA Model Rule 1.7, Comment 20]*

**A16 (H)** A global firm's standard engagement letter asks every client to waive all future conflicts of any kind. The Comment to Rule 1.7 on advance consent says such a waiver is:
a) effective for any client represented by independent counsel b) effective if the letter is signed c) never effective d) ordinarily ineffective when general and open-ended, and more likely effective when specific to a type of conflict the client understands, especially for an experienced or independently advised client — **d.** Comment 22 to Rule 1.7 makes effectiveness turn on the specificity of the consent and the client's sophistication. *[ABA Model Rule 1.7, Comment 22]*

**A17 (M)** A 200-lawyer firm hires a lateral partner who worked adverse to one of the firm's clients at her prior firm. Under ABA Model Rule 1.10(a)(2), the conflict is not imputed to the firm if, among other things, the lateral is timely screened, receives no part of the fee, and:
a) the client of the new firm signs a waiver b) written notice is promptly given to the affected former client describing the screen, with certifications of compliance on request and at the screen's end c) the state bar approves the screen in advance d) the lateral works from a separate office — **b.** ABA Rule 1.10(a)(2) requires prompt written notice and certifications; states differ materially on whether nonconsensual screening is available at all. *[ABA Model Rule 1.10(a)(2); state variants differ, e.g. Minnesota Rule 1.10(b)]*

**A18 (E)** A newly formed 25-lawyer firm asks what "tone at the top" means in control terms. The Green Book principle that covers a commitment to integrity and ethical values, expressed through standards of conduct and the example set by leadership, is:
a) Principle 1 b) Principle 7 c) Principle 11 d) Principle 16 — **a.** Principle 1 requires the oversight body and management to demonstrate commitment to integrity and ethical values. *[GAO Green Book 2025, Principle 1]*

**A19 (M)** A midsize firm is replacing its practice management platform and merging with a five-lawyer boutique in the same quarter. The Green Book principle that asks management to identify, analyze and respond to significant changes that could affect internal control is:
a) Principle 2, exercise oversight responsibility b) Principle 12, implement control activities c) Principle 9, identify, analyze and respond to change d) Principle 14, communicate internally — **c.** Principle 9 covers changes in the external environment, business model and leadership; the 2025 revision also asks for documentation of change assessments. *[GAO Green Book 2025, Principle 9]*

**A20 (H)** A large regional firm's internal audit finds the same docketing deficiency three years in a row and nothing has changed. In Green Book terms the firm is failing the monitoring component, most specifically the principle that management:
a) uses quality information b) designs general controls over information technology c) defines objectives and risk tolerances d) evaluates issues identified through monitoring and remediates deficiencies on a timely basis — **d.** Principle 17 requires evaluation of identified issues and timely remediation; Principle 16 covers performing the monitoring itself. *[GAO Green Book 2025, Principles 16 and 17]*

**A21 (M)** A senior associate is assigned to supervise a litigation paralegal day to day. Under Rule 5.3(b), that associate must:
a) ensure the firm has firm-wide measures for nonlawyer conduct b) make reasonable efforts to ensure the paralegal's conduct is compatible with the lawyer's professional obligations c) do nothing beyond what partners already do under 5.3(a) d) co-sign every document the paralegal prepares — **b.** Rule 5.3(b) places a reasonable-efforts duty on the lawyer with direct supervisory authority; 5.3(a) is the separate firm-level duty. *[ABA Model Rule 5.3(b)]*

**A22 (H)** A midsize firm lets each lawyer decide individually whether and how to use generative AI on client work, with no firm position. ABA Formal Opinion 512 says managerial lawyers should:
a) prohibit generative AI until the ABA issues a further opinion b) leave tool choice to each lawyer's competence judgment c) establish clear policies on permissible use, train subordinates on capabilities, limits and secure data handling, and vet vendors before use d) require client consent in every engagement letter — **c.** Opinion 512 applies Rules 5.1 and 5.3 to require firm policies, training and vendor vetting for generative AI. *[ABA Formal Opinion 512 (2024), supervision section]*

## SECTION B — Trust Accounting & Safekeeping Property (26: 8E/13M/5H)

**B1 (E)** A five-attorney firm deposits retainers into its operating account and tracks them in a spreadsheet. Under Rule 1.15(a), client funds must be held:
a) in any account so long as a ledger is kept b) in the operating account if the amounts are small c) separate from the lawyer's own property, in a separate account d) in a certificate of deposit in the client's name — **c.** Rule 1.15(a) requires client and third-party property to be kept separate from the lawyer's own property. *[ABA Model Rule 1.15(a)]*

**B2 (E)** A solo keeps $2,500 of her own money in the trust account "as a cushion." Under Rule 1.15(b), the lawyer's own funds may be in the trust account only:
a) for the purpose of paying bank service charges, and only in the amount needed for that purpose b) up to ten percent of the balance c) if disclosed to clients in the engagement letter d) if the bank requires a minimum balance — **a.** Rule 1.15(b) limits lawyer funds in trust to what is needed for bank service charges. *[ABA Model Rule 1.15(b)]*

**B3 (E)** A transactional firm collects a $10,000 advance fee and deposits it to operating on the theory that the work will be done within the month. Under Rule 1.15(c), advance fees and expenses are:
a) the lawyer's property on receipt b) held in operating if the engagement letter says so c) held in trust only if the client asks d) deposited into the client trust account and withdrawn only as fees are earned or expenses incurred — **d.** Rule 1.15(c) treats advances as client property until earned. *[ABA Model Rule 1.15(c); California Rule 1.15(b) differs for true flat fees, state-specific]*

**B4 (M)** A settlement check clears in a plaintiff firm's trust account and sits for six weeks before anyone tells the client. Rule 1.15(d) requires the lawyer, on receiving funds in which a client has an interest, to:
a) hold the funds until the client requests them b) promptly notify the client, promptly deliver what the client is entitled to receive, and render a full accounting promptly on request c) transfer the funds to operating pending fee calculation d) notify the client within 90 days — **b.** Rule 1.15(d) imposes prompt notice, prompt delivery and an accounting on request. *[ABA Model Rule 1.15(d)]*

**B5 (M)** A client disputes a firm's fee after a settlement, and the firm freezes the entire settlement in trust until the fee dispute is resolved. Rule 1.15(e) directs that:
a) the entire amount stays in trust until a court rules b) the lawyer may withdraw the fee claimed and hold the rest c) only the disputed portion is kept separate until resolved, and the undisputed portion is distributed promptly d) the funds go to the bar's fee arbitration program — **c.** Rule 1.15(e) separates the disputed portion and requires prompt distribution of what is undisputed. *[ABA Model Rule 1.15(e)]*

**B6 (E)** A small firm shreds trust records two years after each matter closes. The Model Rule default for preserving complete trust records is:
a) five years after termination of the representation, with states free to set longer periods b) one year after the final disbursement c) three years after the account is closed d) ten years in every state — **a.** Rule 1.15(a) uses a bracketed five-year default; states adjust it (for example six years in Minnesota and Florida, seven in New York). *[ABA Model Rule 1.15(a); state variants govern actual period]*

**B7 (M)** A managing partner asks how often the Model Rule requires trust account reconciliation. The accurate answer is that Model Rule 1.15:
a) requires quarterly reconciliation b) requires weekly reconciliation for firms over 20 lawyers c) requires annual reconciliation d) sets no reconciliation frequency and no list of required ledgers; those come from state rules. California, Florida and Minnesota each require monthly reconciliation; New York lists required records but sets no frequency in the rule — **d.** The Model Rule body is silent on frequency and record types, leaving them to state rules, and the states themselves differ: New York Rule 1.15 lists required records without a reconciliation frequency. *[ABA Model Rule 1.15; Cal., N.Y., Fla., Minn. rules supply frequency, state-specific]*

**B8 (E)** A Minnesota firm's bookkeeper compares the bank statement to the check register each month and calls it done. Minnesota's Appendix 1 describes a monthly three-way reconciliation that ties:
a) the operating account, the trust account and the tax return b) the check register, the total of the client subsidiary ledgers and the bank statement c) the fee book, the invoices and the bank statement d) the deposit slips, the cancelled checks and the trial balance — **b.** A two-way match omits the client ledgers; Appendix 1 requires the register, the subsidiary ledger totals and the bank statement to agree. *[Minnesota Rule 1.15(h) and Appendix 1; state-specific]*

**B9 (M)** A Minnesota solo asks how long to keep trust records and whether anyone checks. Under Minnesota Rule 1.15:
a) records are kept three years and audits are random b) records are kept five years after final distribution c) records are kept at least six years after the representation ends, and each lawyer certifies at annual registration that required books and records are maintained d) records are kept seven years and reviewed by the bank — **c.** Minnesota Rule 1.15(h) sets six years; 1.15(i) adds annual certification. *[Minnesota Rule 1.15(h) and (i); state-specific]*

**B10 (M)** A Minnesota firm's trust account has an item presented against insufficient funds, and the bank honors it. Under Minnesota Rule 1.15, the bank:
a) reports the event to the Office of Lawyers Professional Responsibility, and honored items are reported within five banking days b) reports only if the lawyer authorizes it c) reports only unpaid items d) has no reporting role; the lawyer self-reports — **a.** Minnesota Rule 1.15(k) through (n) require approved institutions to report insufficient-funds events, and every practicing lawyer consents to that reporting. *[Minnesota Rule 1.15(k) to (n); state-specific]*

**B11 (M)** A Minnesota firm keeps $1,500 of its own money in trust to cover fees and charges. Minnesota's Appendix 1 contemplates a ledger for the lawyer's nominal funds capped at:
a) $1,000 b) $5,000 c) whatever the bank requires d) $200 — **d.** Appendix 1 to Minnesota Rule 1.15 caps the lawyer's nominal funds ledger at $200. *[Minnesota Appendix 1 to Rule 1.15; state-specific]*

**B12 (H)** A California firm charges a $1,500 true flat fee for an uncontested matter and deposits it directly to operating. Under California Rule 1.15(b), a flat fee paid in advance may go to the operating account only:
a) if the firm has fewer than five lawyers b) with written disclosure that the client may require trust deposit until earned and is entitled to a refund of any unearned portion, and, for flat fees over $1,000, with the client's signed written agreement to those terms c) if the work is completed within 30 days d) never; California requires all advances in trust — **b.** California Rule 1.15(b) departs from Model Rule 1.15(c) for true flat fees, with disclosure and a signed writing above $1,000. *[California Rule 1.15(b); state-specific]*

**B13 (M)** A California solo has never heard of CTAPP. Under the State Bar's Client Trust Account Protection Program, licensees must annually:
a) submit their trust records to the bar for review b) obtain a CPA opinion on the trust account c) register their IOLTA and non-IOLTA trust accounts, complete a self-assessment of trust practices, and certify their understanding of Rule 1.15 d) close any account that held funds for more than one year — **c.** CTAPP annual reporting combines account registration, self-assessment and certification. *[State Bar of California, CTAPP annual reporting; state-specific]*

**B14 (H)** A California firm receives notice that it has been selected for a CTAPP compliance review. Based on the program launched September 29, 2025, the review:
a) is conducted by a trained CPA firm on randomly selected licensees and examines recordkeeping, notification and distribution timeframes, supervision, monthly three-way reconciliations, client ledgers and trust journals b) is triggered only by a client complaint c) is conducted by bar staff and limited to the IOLTA account d) applies only to firms over 50 lawyers — **a.** The CTAPP compliance review program uses random selection and CPA reviewers, with cost exemptions for low-grossing attorneys. *[State Bar of California news release, CTAPP compliance reviews, Sept. 2025; state-specific]*

**B15 (M)** A New York firm's escrow account statements are shredded after five years. New York Rule 1.15(d) requires the enumerated bookkeeping records to be kept for:
a) three years b) five years c) six years d) seven years, with contemporaneous and accurate entries — **d.** New York Rule 1.15(d) sets a seven-year retention period and requires contemporaneous entries. *[22 NYCRR 1200, Rule 1.15(d); state-specific]*

**B16 (H)** A New York firm's office manager, who is not a lawyer, is a signatory on the attorney special account and makes cash withdrawals for client payouts. Under New York Rule 1.15(e):
a) a nonlawyer may sign if bonded b) only lawyers admitted in New York may be signatories, withdrawals must be to a named payee rather than in cash, and electronic transfers require prior written approval c) cash withdrawals are permitted under $1,000 d) any firm employee may sign with a partner's written authorization — **b.** New York Rule 1.15(e) restricts signatories to New York-admitted lawyers and prohibits cash withdrawals. *[22 NYCRR 1200, Rule 1.15(e); state-specific]*

**B17 (M)** A Florida firm reconciles its trust account each quarter and has never filed anything with The Florida Bar about it. Florida Rule 5-1.2 minimum procedures require:
a) quarterly reconciliation and no filing b) an annual CPA audit c) monthly reconciliation of each trust account against the total of client ledger balances, an annual listing of unexpended balances per client, and a compliance certificate filed between June 1 and August 15 each year d) weekly reconciliation for contingency practices — **c.** Florida Rule 5-1.2 sets monthly reconciliation, an annual listing and an annual certificate. *[Rules Regulating The Florida Bar, Rule 5-1.2 (Dec. 2025); state-specific]*

**B18 (H)** A Florida solo's trust check is dishonored and she fails to file the annual compliance certificate. Under Florida Rule 5-1.2, these events:
a) are among the triggers for a Bar-ordered trust audit, and the lawyer bears the audit cost only if substantial noncompliance is found b) result in automatic suspension c) require a CPA opinion within 30 days d) are handled solely through the bank's overdraft reporting — **a.** Florida lists dishonored checks and certificate failures among audit triggers and shifts cost only on substantial noncompliance. *[Rules Regulating The Florida Bar, Rule 5-1.2 audits; state-specific]*

**B19 (E)** A small firm holds many small, short-term client deposits and asks why the interest does not go to each client. Under the IOLTA rules of the four states in this bank, funds that are nominal in amount or held for a short period:
a) earn interest for the firm's operating account b) must be placed in separate accounts for each client c) may not be held in an interest-bearing account d) are pooled in an IOLTA account whose net interest is remitted to a legal services program administered in that state — **d.** California (B&P 6211), New York (IOLA), Minnesota (Rule 1.15(e) and (o)) and Florida (IOTA, Rule 5-1.1) all pool nominal or short-term funds for legal aid. *[Cal. B&P 6211; N.Y. Rule 1.15(b); Minn. Rule 1.15(e), (o); Fla. Rule 5-1.1; state-specific in each case]*

**B20 (M)** A Minnesota firm receives a $900,000 escrow it will hold for 14 months and puts it in the pooled IOLTA account. Minnesota Rule 1.15(f) and (g) contemplate that larger or longer-held funds:
a) must stay in the IOLTA account b) may be placed in a separate interest-bearing account for the client, or a pooled account with subaccounting, considering earnings, administrative cost and bank capability c) must be returned to the client until closing d) must be held by an escrow company — **b.** Minnesota 1.15(f) permits separate interest-bearing accounts for the client and 1.15(g) lists the selection factors. *[Minnesota Rule 1.15(f) and (g); state-specific]*

**B21 (M)** In a three-attorney firm, one bookkeeper enters ledger transactions, prepares checks and performs the monthly reconciliation. Adapting the Green Book's approach to segregation of duties, the firm should:
a) suspend reconciliation until it can hire a second bookkeeper b) accept the risk in writing and take no other step c) design compensating controls, for example a partner receiving bank statements directly, approving disbursements and reviewing the reconciliation, and document the trade-off d) outsource the trust account to the bank — **c.** Green Book application guidance expects management to design compensating controls and document how objectives are met where full segregation is not feasible. *[GAO Green Book 2025, Principle 10 and application guidance]*

**B22 (E)** A firm's pooled trust account holds funds for 40 clients. The record that shows how much of the pooled balance belongs to each client is the:
a) subsidiary ledger per client matter b) check register c) bank statement d) fee book — **a.** Every state rule in this bank requires a per-client ledger; the register and statement show only the aggregate. *[Minn. Appendix 1; Cal. Rule 1.15(e) Standards; Fla. Rule 5-1.2; N.Y. Rule 1.15(d); state-specific; ABA Model Rule 1.15(a) complete records]*

**B23 (M)** A midsize firm's trust disbursement to a "new" vendor account turns out to have followed a spoofed email. The Green Book principle that asks management to consider the potential for fraud, including improper payments, when identifying risks is:
a) Principle 2 b) Principle 4 c) Principle 14 d) Principle 8 — **d.** Principle 8 covers assessing fraud, improper payment and information security risk; the 2025 revision adds the improper payment and information security emphasis. *[GAO Green Book 2025, Principle 8]*

**B24 (H)** A client discharges a small firm midway through a matter with $4,000 of an advance fee unearned. Reading Rule 1.16(d) together with Rule 1.15(c), on termination the firm must:
a) apply the balance to a termination fee b) refund the unearned advance fee, which was client property held in trust until earned c) hold the balance until the client signs a release d) transfer the balance to successor counsel automatically — **b.** Rule 1.16(d) requires refund of any advance payment of fee not earned; 1.15(c) is why the funds were in trust. *[ABA Model Rule 1.16(d); ABA Model Rule 1.15(c)]*

**B25 (E)** A Minnesota firm keeps its trust books only inside a cloud accounting application with no separate copies. Minnesota's Appendix 1 states that computer-maintained trust records:
a) need no backup if the vendor is reputable b) must be printed and mailed to the client quarterly c) should be regularly backed up and retained as monthly printouts or PDF copies on a separate device d) may be kept without backup for firms under five lawyers — **c.** Appendix 1 to Minnesota Rule 1.15 addresses backup frequency and monthly printouts or PDF retention on a separate device. *[Minnesota Appendix 1 to Rule 1.15; state-specific]*

**B26 (M)** A firm with offices in two states keeps all client trust funds in a bank in the state where its larger office sits. Under Rule 1.15(a), client funds are kept in a separate account maintained:
a) in the state where the lawyer's office is situated, or elsewhere with the consent of the client or third person b) in the state of the client's residence c) in any federally insured bank in any state d) in the state where the matter is pending — **a.** Rule 1.15(a) ties the account location to the office, with a consent exception. *[ABA Model Rule 1.15(a); N.Y. ## SECTION C — Confidentiality, Security Controls, Incident Response & Breach Duties (24: 7E/12M/5H)

**C1 (E)** A solo asks what standard governs her obligation to protect client information from hackers. Rule 1.6(c) requires a lawyer to:
a) guarantee that client information is never accessed without authorization b) make reasonable efforts to prevent inadvertent or unauthorized disclosure of, or unauthorized access to, information relating to the representation c) encrypt every file and every email d) obtain cyber insurance — **b.** Rule 1.6(c), added in 2012, states a reasonable-efforts standard. *[ABA Model Rule 1.6(c)]*

**C2 (E)** A midsize firm wants to decide how much security a matter needs. The Comment to Rule 1.6 lists factors for whether efforts are reasonable, including all of the following except:
a) the sensitivity of the information b) the likelihood of disclosure if additional safeguards are not used c) the cost and difficulty of additional safeguards d) the size of the client's legal budget — **d.** Comment 18 lists sensitivity, likelihood of disclosure, cost, difficulty of implementation and adverse effect on the representation. *[ABA Model Rule 1.6, Comment 18]*

**C3 (M)** A 40-lawyer firm with documented, reasonable safeguards suffers a breach through a previously unknown software flaw. Under the Comment to Rule 1.6:
a) the unauthorized access does not by itself constitute a violation if the lawyer made reasonable efforts to prevent it b) any breach is a per se violation c) the breach is a violation unless the firm carried cyber insurance d) the breach is a violation only for the partner in charge of IT — **a.** Comment 18 states that unauthorized access does not itself constitute a violation where reasonable efforts were made. *[ABA Model Rule 1.6, Comment 18]*

**C4 (M)** A corporate client's outside counsel guidelines require encrypted communications on all matters, while another client asks the firm to send drafts by ordinary email to save time. The Comment to Rule 1.6 says:
a) the lawyer must apply one firm-wide standard to all clients b) client instructions cannot change the lawyer's security obligations c) a client may require special security measures not otherwise required, or may give informed consent to forgo measures the rule would otherwise require d) the more restrictive client's standard applies to all clients — **c.** Comment 18 expressly permits clients to raise or, by informed consent, lower the safeguards. *[ABA Model Rule 1.6, Comment 18]*

**C5 (M)** A small firm sends routine scheduling emails to clients without encryption. The Comment to Rule 1.6 on transmitting communications says special security measures are:
a) required for every client communication b) not required when the method of communication affords a reasonable expectation of privacy, though special circumstances may warrant more c) required only for litigation matters d) required only when the client is a business — **b.** Comment 19 ties the precaution level to the reasonable expectation of privacy, with special circumstances (including other law) warranting more. *[ABA Model Rule 1.6, Comment 19]*

**C6 (M)** A firm's policy states that unencrypted email is always acceptable because a 1999 ABA opinion said so. ABA Formal Opinion 477R says:
a) unencrypted email is never acceptable after 2017 b) email must always be replaced by a client portal c) only encrypted email satisfies Rule 1.6(c) d) unencrypted email remains acceptable for routine matters when basic security measures are in place, but is not always reasonable, and for highly sensitive information the lawyer should discuss whether another mode is warranted — **d.** Opinion 477R replaces a blanket rule with a case-by-case analysis under the Comment 18 factors. *[ABA Formal Opinion 477R (2017)]*

**C7 (H)** A large regional firm's security lead asks whether Opinion 477R prescribes specific controls. The opinion's approach is best described as:
a) process-based: understand the threat, map where client information is stored and transmitted, use reasonable measures, decide how communications are protected, label confidential information, train staff, and conduct vendor due diligence, reassessed over time b) a fixed checklist of required technologies c) a rule that firewalls and passwords are sufficient d) a rule deferring entirely to state bar guidance — **a.** Opinion 477R declines to prescribe specific measures and instead sets out seven considerations within a risk assessment cycle. *[ABA Formal Opinion 477R (2017), seven considerations]*

**C8 (E)** After a ransomware event, a small firm's partners debate whether to tell clients. ABA Formal Opinion 483 says a lawyer must notify current clients under Rule 1.4 when:
a) law enforcement asks the firm to do so b) the firm's insurer approves the notice c) material client confidential information has been, or is reasonably suspected to have been, compromised d) more than 500 clients are affected — **c.** Opinion 483 keys client notice to compromise, or reasonable suspicion of compromise, of material client confidential information. *[ABA Formal Opinion 483 (2018)]*

**C9 (M)** The same firm asks whether Opinion 483 requires it to notify clients whose matters closed years ago. The opinion:
a) requires notice to all former clients b) declines to impose an ethics duty to notify former clients absent a black-letter rule, while recommending retention policies that limit exposure and noting that statutory breach laws apply separately c) requires notice to former clients only if the firm still holds their files d) treats former and current clients identically — **b.** Opinion 483 limits the ethics-based notice duty to current clients and points to statutes for other obligations. *[ABA Formal Opinion 483 (2018); state breach and notice rules vary, state-specific]*

**C10 (M)** A firm's files are encrypted by malware for four days with no evidence of exfiltration, and the partners conclude Opinion 483 does not apply because nothing was "stolen." The opinion's definition of a data breach:
a) is limited to theft of data b) is limited to events involving personal information c) requires a regulator's determination d) also covers episodes where the lawyer's ability to perform the legal services for which the lawyer was hired is significantly impaired — **d.** Opinion 483 defines a breach to include misappropriation, destruction or compromise of material client information, or significant impairment of the lawyer's ability to perform. *[ABA Formal Opinion 483 (2018), scope definition]*

**C11 (H)** A midsize firm has no incident response plan and does not review its systems for intrusions. Reading Opinion 483 on duties that arise before any breach, the firm should:
a) use reasonable efforts to monitor for breaches and proactively develop an incident response plan that assigns roles and covers identifying, assessing, containing, eradicating and restoring b) wait for a breach and then engage counsel c) rely on its cloud provider's monitoring d) adopt a plan only if a client requires one — **a.** Opinion 483 reads Rules 1.1, 1.6(c), 5.1 and 5.3 to require monitoring and recommends a plan with specific procedures and assigned roles. *[ABA Formal Opinion 483 (2018), pre-breach duties]*

**C12 (E)** A firm adopting NIST CSF 2.0 asks how many Functions it has and which one is new. CSF 2.0 has:
a) five Functions, with Recover new b) four Functions, with Protect new c) six Functions (Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover), with Govern new in 2.0 d) seven Functions, with Supply Chain new — **c.** NIST CSF 2.0 (February 2024) added Govern to the five Functions of CSF 1.1. *[NIST CSF 2.0, CSWP 29 (2024)]*

**C13 (E)** A firm wants to map its written security policy to CSF 2.0. The Govern Category that covers establishing, communicating and enforcing policy is:
a) GV.OC b) GV.PO c) GV.RM d) GV.OV — **b.** GV.PO is the Policy Category; GV.OC is organizational context, GV.RM risk management strategy and GV.OV oversight. *[NIST CSF 2.0, GV.PO]*

**C14 (M)** During an incident, a firm needs a defined process for who tells clients, regulators and its insurer, and when. The CSF 2.0 Category that covers incident response reporting and communication is:
a) DE.AE b) PR.AT c) ID.RA d) RS.CO — **d.** RS.CO, under Respond, covers coordination and communication with internal and external stakeholders during response. *[NIST CSF 2.0, RS.CO]*

**C15 (M)** A firm tests whether it can actually rebuild its document system from backups after an outage. The CSF 2.0 Category most directly exercised is:
a) RC.RP, incident recovery plan execution b) GV.SC, supply chain c) DE.CM, continuous monitoring d) PR.AT, awareness and training — **a.** RC.RP covers execution of the recovery plan to restore affected assets and operations. *[NIST CSF 2.0, RC.RP]*

**C16 (M)** A small firm's document system accepts a password alone. The SP 800-53 Rev. 5 controls that call for multi-factor authentication for privileged and non-privileged accounts are:
a) AC-2 and AC-6 b) AU-2 and AU-6 c) IA-2(1) and IA-2(2) d) CP-9 and CP-10 — **c.** IA-2(1) covers MFA to privileged accounts and IA-2(2) MFA to non-privileged accounts. *[NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5, IA-2(1) and IA-2(2)]*

**C17 (E)** Every user at a 30-lawyer firm has administrator rights on the practice management system. The SP 800-53 control that limits access to what users need for their assigned tasks is:
a) SC-7 b) AC-6, least privilege c) MP-6 d) SI-2 — **b.** AC-6 permits only the accesses necessary to accomplish assigned tasks. *[NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5, AC-6]*

**C18 (M)** A paralegal leaves a midsize firm on Friday and her remote access still works the following Wednesday. The SP 800-53 controls most directly implicated are:
a) CM-3 and CM-8 b) RA-3 and RA-5 c) SC-8 and SC-28 d) PS-4 (personnel termination) and AC-2(3) (disable accounts) — **d.** PS-4 requires disabling access and revoking authenticators on termination; AC-2(3) requires disabling accounts no longer associated with a user. *[NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5, PS-4 and AC-2(3)]*

**C19 (M)** A firm's systems generate logs that no one has ever read. The SP 800-53 control that requires reviewing and analyzing audit records at a defined frequency for unusual activity is:
a) AU-6 b) AU-2 c) AU-12 d) SI-3 — **a.** AU-2 selects the events to log and AU-12 generates the records; AU-6 is the review and analysis control. *[NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5, AU-6]*

**C20 (H)** A large regional firm has a written incident response plan that has never been exercised. Under SP 800-53, the plan document and its periodic testing map to two different controls:
a) IR-4 and IR-6 b) IR-6 and IR-8 c) IR-8 (incident response plan) and IR-3 (incident response testing, such as tabletop exercises) d) IR-3 and IR-4 — **c.** IR-8 governs the plan; IR-3 requires testing the capability at a defined frequency using defined tests. *[NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5, IR-8 and IR-3]*

**C21 (H)** A firm's password policy requires a change every 60 days, complexity rules and an 8-character minimum for single-factor logins. Under SP 800-63B-4, the practices that conflict with the standard are:
a) the 60-day change only b) forced periodic change and composition rules, both of which the standard says shall not be imposed; the single-factor minimum is also short of the 15-character floor c) none; all three practices are required d) the 8-character minimum only — **b.** SP 800-63B-4 prohibits periodic rotation and composition rules, sets 15 characters as the single-factor minimum and 8 when used within MFA. *[NIST SP 800-63B-4 (2025), Section 3.1.1]*

**C22 (M)** A firm reasons that because the practice of law is outside the federal GLBA rules, no breach notification law applies to it. The accurate position is that:
a) law firms are exempt from all breach notification statutes b) only firms over 100 lawyers are covered c) breach notification applies only to firms with government clients d) every state, the District of Columbia and several territories have breach notification statutes that reach any entity holding covered personal information, and the deadlines and thresholds vary by state — **d.** State breach statutes apply independently of the GLBA outcome; the specific timing and content rules are state-specific. *[NCSL, Security Breach Notification Laws (CITED); state-specific]*

**C23 (H)** A 20-lawyer firm's consultant says the FTC Safeguards Rule, 16 CFR Part 314, legally requires the firm to appoint a Qualified Individual. The accurate position is that:
a) the practice of law was held not to be a "financial institution" under GLBA in ABA v. FTC (D.C. Cir. 2005), so Part 314 binds a firm only through a genuinely financial affiliate, though its elements remain a useful benchmark b) Part 314 binds every law firm since the 2023 amendments c) Part 314 binds firms that handle real estate closings d) Part 314 binds firms with more than 5,000 clients — **a.** The D.C. Circuit rejected the FTC's reading; the Rule's written program, MFA, encryption and vendor oversight elements are benchmarks, not obligations, for the practice of law. *[16 CFR 314; ABA v. FTC, 430 F.3d 457 (D.C. Cir. 2005)]*

**C24 (E)** A firm donates old laptops to a school after deleting the files. The SP 800-53 control that requires media to be sanitized before disposal, release or reuse is:
a) SC-28 b) CP-9 c) MP-6, media sanitization d) AC-17 — **c.** MP-6 requires sanitization using approved techniques before media leaves organizational control; Opinion 477R likewise notes that "delete" does not permanently erase data. *[NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5, MP-6]*

## SECTION D — Vendor Risk, Cloud, Outsourcing & Business Continuity (18: 5E/9M/4H)

**D1 (E)** A solo moves all client files to an internet-based storage service and assumes ethics rules stop at the firm's door. The Comment to Rule 5.3 says that when using outside nonlawyer services such as internet-based data storage, the lawyer must:
a) obtain the client's written consent in every matter b) make reasonable efforts to ensure the services are provided in a manner compatible with the lawyer's professional obligations c) use only providers approved by the state bar d) keep a paper copy of every file — **b.** Comment 3 to Rule 5.3 extends the reasonable-efforts duty to outside services and names internet-based data storage. *[ABA Model Rule 5.3, Comment 3]*

**D2 (M)** A midsize firm wants a defensible way to decide how much oversight each vendor needs. The Comment to Rule 5.3 says the extent of the obligation depends on:
a) the vendor's price and contract length b) whether the vendor is domestic c) the number of lawyers using the service d) the education, experience and reputation of the nonlawyer, the nature of the services, the terms of any arrangement concerning protection of client information, and the legal and ethical environment of the jurisdiction where the services are performed — **d.** Comment 3 to Rule 5.3 lists these factors and adds that the lawyer should give appropriate instructions. *[ABA Model Rule 5.3, Comment 3]*

**D3 (M)** A corporate client directs its outside counsel to use the client's chosen e-discovery vendor. The Comment to Rule 5.3 says the lawyer should ordinarily:
a) agree with the client on how responsibility for monitoring the vendor will be allocated, consistent with Rule 1.2 b) decline the engagement c) treat the vendor as the client's agent with no lawyer oversight d) supervise the vendor exactly as if the firm had hired it — **a.** Comment 4 to Rule 5.3 addresses client-selected providers and allocation of monitoring responsibility. *[ABA Model Rule 5.3, Comment 4]*

**D4 (E)** A plaintiff firm outsources document review to an off-site contract review company. ABA Formal Opinion 08-451 says that outsourcing is:
a) prohibited for legal work b) permitted only with bar approval c) permitted, with the lawyer remaining ultimately responsible for competent representation and for compliance with the Rules d) permitted only for nonlegal support — **c.** Opinion 08-451 permits outsourcing of legal and nonlegal support while keeping responsibility with the lawyer. *[ABA Formal Opinion 08-451 (2008)]*

**D5 (M)** The same firm plans to give the review company access to privileged client documents and will not closely supervise its staff. Under Opinion 08-451, the firm should:
a) proceed if the vendor signs a confidentiality agreement b) obtain the client's informed consent, because outsiders will access Rule 1.6 information and the relationship lacks the close supervision typical of firm employees c) proceed if the reviewers are licensed lawyers d) proceed if the work is billed as a disbursement — **b.** Opinion 08-451 requires disclosure and informed consent where outsourced personnel access confidential information or supervision is not close. *[ABA Formal Opinion 08-451 (2008)]*

**D6 (H)** A firm pays a contract review provider $40 per hour and bills the client $110 per hour as a "cost." Under Opinion 08-451's fee analysis:
a) any markup is permitted if the total is reasonable b) markup is never permitted on outsourced work c) markup is permitted only for offshore providers d) if passed through as a disbursement, the charge is limited to actual cost plus a reasonable overhead allocation absent client agreement; a markup is allowed only where the work is billed as legal fees — **d.** Opinion 08-451 distinguishes work billed as fees (reasonable markup permitted) from disbursements (cost plus overhead absent agreement). *[ABA Formal Opinion 08-451 (2008), Rule 1.5 discussion]*

**D7 (M)** A midsize firm considers an overseas provider for legal research support. Opinion 08-451 adds, for foreign providers, due diligence on:
a) whether legal education is comparable to U.S. standards, whether the disciplinary system is effective, the risk of document seizure, and whether courts there will protect client information b) the provider's marketing claims c) currency exchange risk only d) the number of U.S. clients the provider has — **a.** Opinion 08-451 lists additional factors for foreign providers beyond the general reference checks, facility security and disposal practices. *[ABA Formal Opinion 08-451 (2008), due diligence factors]*

**D8 (E)** A firm reviews its cloud provider's independent security report before renewal. In CSF 2.0 terms, the firm is exercising the Govern Category for:
a) GV.OC, organizational context b) GV.RR, roles and responsibilities c) GV.SC, cybersecurity supply chain risk management d) GV.OV, oversight — **c.** GV.SC covers identifying, establishing, managing and monitoring supply chain risk processes, including supplier due diligence. *[NIST CSF 2.0, GV.SC]*

**D9 (M)** A large regional firm assessed its vendors once at onboarding and never again. The SP 800-53 control that calls for assessing and reviewing supply chain risks associated with suppliers at a defined frequency is:
a) SA-9 only b) SR-6, supplier assessments and reviews, alongside SA-9's requirements for external service providers c) CM-8 d) PS-3 — **b.** SR-6 adds periodic review; SA-9 sets the security requirements and oversight roles for external services. *[NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5, SR-6 and SA-9]*

**D10 (M)** A global firm wants a federal reference for building a supply chain risk program across firm leadership, practice groups and IT. The NIST publication dedicated to cybersecurity supply chain risk management practices, using a multilevel approach with strategy, policy and assessment guidance, is:
a) SP 800-63B b) SP 800-34 c) SP 800-171 d) SP 800-161 Rev. 1 (May 2022) — **d.** SP 800-161 Rev. 1 is NIST's C-SCRM practices guide for systems and organizations. *[NIST SP 800-161 Rev. 1 (2022)]*

**D11 (H)** A firm's contract with a generative AI vendor is silent on what happens to client data when the engagement ends. Ethics guidance on vendor terms says the firm should check, among other things:
a) data retention and ownership after the engagement, breach notification rights, the vendor's confidentiality commitments, and how data can be retrieved or destroyed if the vendor fails or the relationship ends b) only the subscription price and term c) only whether the vendor has a privacy policy d) only whether the vendor is headquartered in the United States — **a.** Opinion 512 lists retention and ownership after the engagement, breach notification and legal remedies; North Carolina's opinion adds data retrieval and destruction if the vendor fails. *[ABA Formal Opinion 512 (2024), vendor section; N.C. 2024 FEO 1, state-specific]*

**D12 (M)** A small firm asks whether state bars agree on cloud storage of client files. The state cloud opinions surveyed:
a) split evenly on whether cloud storage is permitted b) uniformly prohibit cloud storage for litigation files c) uniformly permit cloud use with reasonable care to protect confidentiality, and diverge mainly on whether client consent should be obtained and how much vendor diligence is expected d) require bar pre-approval of each provider — **c.** More than 20 state opinions reach the same permission-with-reasonable-care result; consent is the point of divergence. *[Illinois State Bar Op. 16-06 and comparable state cloud opinions (CITED); state-specific]*

**D13 (E)** A solo practitioner with 80 open matters has made no arrangement for what happens if she dies or becomes disabled. The Comment to Rule 1.3 says a sole practitioner should:
a) rely on the state bar to appoint a trustee b) notify clients only if a diagnosis is received c) keep paper files so a family member can find them d) prepare a plan that designates another competent lawyer to review client files, notify each client of the lawyer's death or disability, and determine whether immediate protective action is needed — **d.** Comment 5 to Rule 1.3 describes the succession plan expected of a sole practitioner. *[ABA Model Rule 1.3, Comment 5]*

**D14 (M)** A New York solo is the only signatory on his attorney trust account. Under New York Rule 1.15, if the sole signatory dies:
a) the court may appoint a successor signatory on application b) the account is frozen until probate closes c) the bank releases funds to the estate d) the Lawyers' Fund takes all balances automatically — **a.** New York Rule 1.15(g) provides for court appointment of a successor signatory. *[22 NYCRR 1200, Rule 1.15(g); state-specific]*

**D15 (H)** A retiring Minnesota solo sells her entire practice to a two-lawyer firm. Under Minnesota Rule 1.17, the seller must give each client written notice of the sale, the buyer's identity and background, and the client's right to retain other counsel or take the file, and:
a) each client must sign before any file transfers b) if served personally or by certified mail, a client's silence for 90 days may be deemed consent, and the buyer may not raise fees by reason of the sale for at least one year c) clients have 30 days to object d) the bar must approve the buyer — **b.** Minnesota Rule 1.17 uses a 90-day deemed consent and a one-year fee freeze; the ABA version differs in details. *[Minnesota Rule 1.17; state-specific; ABA Model Rule 1.17 differs]*

**D16 (M)** A 40-lawyer firm needs to decide which systems to restore first after an outage and how much data loss it can tolerate. The NIST contingency planning guide that introduces the business impact analysis and the recovery time and recovery point objectives is:
a) SP 800-53 AU family b) SP 800-63B c) SP 800-34 Rev. 1 d) SP 800-161 — **c.** SP 800-34 Rev. 1 covers the BIA, RTO and RPO, plan development and testing for information system contingency planning. *[NIST SP 800-34 Rev. 1 (2010)]*

**D17 (E)** A small firm's only backup is a copy synced to the same cloud account that its production files live in. The SP 800-53 control on system backup, read with CISA's small business guidance, expects a firm to:
a) keep backups only on the office server b) back up quarterly c) keep backups only for the trust ledger d) conduct backups of user and system information and protect their confidentiality, integrity and availability, with CISA adding regular automated backups and offline copies — **d.** CP-9 sets the backup control; CISA Cyber Essentials' "Your Data" element adds automated backups, redundancy and offline copies. *[NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5, CP-9; CISA Cyber Essentials (2019)]*

**D18 (H)** A firm drafting a cloud vendor questionnaire asks what topics a state bar has listed as reasonable inquiries. The Illinois State Bar opinion on cloud storage lists, among others:
a) security precautions, provider reputation, breach history, a confidentiality agreement with breach notification, the firm's control over backup and retrieval, and provisions for termination or the provider's business closure b) the provider's stock price and staff headcount c) whether the provider offers a free tier d) whether the provider hosts other law firms — **a.** ISBA Opinion 16-06 gives a non-exhaustive list that maps to the Rule 5.3 Comment 3 factors. *[Illinois State Bar Ass'n Op. 16-06 (CITED); state-specific]*
